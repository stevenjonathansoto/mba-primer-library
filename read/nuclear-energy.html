INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Nuclear energy is experiencing its most significant renaissance since the 1970s — driven not by government mandates, but by Big Tech's insatiable demand for 24/7 carbon-free baseload power to fuel the AI revolution. For MBA candidates, nuclear sits at the intersection of energy security, geopolitics, advanced manufacturing, and infrastructure finance in ways no other energy technology does. Understanding it means understanding why Microsoft paid $16 billion to restart Three Mile Island, why Meta committed to 6.6 GW of nuclear capacity, and why Small Modular Reactors — factory-built, modular, faster to deploy — could be the defining clean energy technology of the 2030s.

HISTORY & ORIGINS

The nuclear age began December 2, 1942, when Enrico Fermi's team achieved the first controlled nuclear chain reaction at the University of Chicago's CP-1 reactor. Within a decade, the technology had both destroyed two cities and offered the promise of "electricity too cheap to meter." The history of nuclear energy is a story of extraordinary technological achievement, catastrophic accidents, political reversals, and now — perhaps — genuine revival.

1954–1979: The First Nuclear Age.  The world's first civilian nuclear power plant opened at Obninsk, USSR in 1954. The U.S. launched its civilian program with Shippingport (1958), and the "Atoms for Peace" era drove rapid construction. By the late 1970s, hundreds of reactors were operating or under construction globally. The combination of the 1973 oil crisis (which made nuclear look strategically attractive) and the 1979 Three Mile Island accident (which crystallized public fear) defined the era's contradictions. No new U.S. reactors were ordered after 1978.

1986–2000: Chernobyl and the Long Stagnation.  The 1986 Chernobyl disaster in the Soviet Ukraine killed 31 people directly and contaminated vast areas of Europe, becoming the defining nuclear accident in public consciousness. In combination with TMI, it ended nuclear's growth trajectory in Western nations. Germany began planning its nuclear exit. The U.S., UK, and most of Europe cancelled planned reactors. Only France (75% nuclear electricity by the 1990s), Japan, South Korea, and China continued meaningful programs.

2000–2011: The “Nuclear Renaissance” That Wasn’t.  2000–2011: The "Nuclear Renaissance" That Wasn't. Rising oil prices, climate concerns, and energy security anxiety produced the first "nuclear renaissance" rhetoric in the 2000s. New reactor orders were placed in the U.S. (Vogtle, Summer), UK, and elsewhere. The 2008 financial crisis raised capital costs. Then the March 2011 Fukushima Daiichi disaster — triggered by a tsunami — caused three reactor meltdowns and the release of radiation, prompting Germany to immediately shut 8 reactors and commit to full nuclear exit by 2022, and Japan to idle its entire fleet for years.

2012–2022: Vogtle and the Cost Problem.  The U.S. construction of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia became the cautionary tale for large nuclear economics: originally budgeted at $14B, the project ultimately cost $35B+ and ran 7 years late. Only South Korea and China demonstrated the ability to build large reactors on time and on budget. Meanwhile, the IRA (2022) included nuclear PTCs for the first time, signaling policy support — but the economics of new large nuclear remained challenged in most Western markets.

2023–2026: The AI-Driven Revival.  The convergence of AI data center power demand, climate commitments, and SMR technology maturation has produced a genuine nuclear revival — one driven by private capital rather than government mandates. Microsoft's 20-year PPA to restart Three Mile Island (2024, DOE loan closed 2025) was the catalyst. Google, Amazon, and Meta followed with their own nuclear deals totaling 9.7+ GW. The IEA declared 2025 a "structural turning point" for nuclear, with record electricity generation and 70+ GW under construction globally.

MARKET STRUCTURE & VALUE CHAIN

The nuclear fuel cycle is the most complex value chain in energy — spanning mining, conversion, enrichment, fuel fabrication, reactor operation, and waste management across a 60–80 year asset life. Each stage requires specialized technology, regulatory approval, and geopolitical navigation. Russia's dominance of enrichment and HALEU fuel supply is the nuclear industry's equivalent of China's dominance of solar panel manufacturing.

The Nuclear Value Chain (Upstream → Downstream)

1. Uranium Mining

Yellowcake (U₃O₈) produced from uranium ore. Top producers: Kazakhstan (43% of world supply, Kazatomprom), Canada (Cameco), Australia, Namibia. Uranium spot price surged from ~$25/lb (2020) to ~$100+/lb (2024) before settling; supply concentration a strategic risk.

2. Conversion

Yellowcake converted to uranium hexafluoride (UF₆) for enrichment. Key facilities: Blind River/Port Hope (Canada, Cameco), Honeywell (USA), Orano (France), Rosatom (Russia). Russia accounts for ~30% of global conversion capacity.

3. Enrichment

Natural uranium (0.7% U-235) enriched to 3–5% (LEU) for conventional reactors, or 5–20% (HALEU) for advanced SMRs. Russia (Rosatom/TVEL) dominates with ~40% of global enrichment capacity. U.S. has UUSA and Centrus; EU has Urenco. HALEU supply is the critical bottleneck for advanced SMRs.

4. Fuel Fabrication

LEU/HALEU converted into fuel rods and assemblies. Westinghouse (USA, now owned by Brookfield), Framatome (France), Rosatom (Russia), CANDU (Canada), GE Hitachi (USA/Japan). Each reactor design requires specific fuel geometry.

5. Reactor Design & OEM

Light water reactors (LWR): Westinghouse AP1000, GE Hitachi BWRX-300, EDF EPR. Advanced SMRs: NuScale VOYGR, X-energy Xe-100, TerraPower Natrium, Kairos Power KP-FHR. Korea (KEPCO) and China (CNEC, CGN) are increasingly competitive for international large-reactor projects.

6. EPC & Construction

Engineering, procurement, construction of reactor buildings, containment structures, turbines. Historically massive cost and schedule overruns in Western markets (Vogtle, Hinkley Point C). South Korea and China demonstrate on-time, on-budget delivery. SMRs promise factory-built modules reducing construction risk.

7. Plant Operations & O&M

Reactor operations, fuel management, safety systems, grid dispatch. U.S. fleet: Constellation Energy, Vistra, Entergy, Duke, NextEra. Capacity factors of 90%+ make nuclear the highest-performing fleet by utilization. Staffing: 400–700 specialized employees per plant.

8. Waste Management & Decommissioning

Spent nuclear fuel (SNF) stored on-site in cooling pools then dry casks globally — no permanent disposal facility operational anywhere. U.S. Yucca Mountain remains blocked. Decommissioning costs: $300–700M per reactor. Long-term waste management is the industry's unresolved liability.

Three market segments define nuclear deployment:

Large-Scale Conventional Reactors (1,000–1,700 MWe): The established technology (PWR, BWR, CANDU). Baseload workhorses with 60–80 year design lives. New builds are primarily in China (25+ under construction), India, South Korea, and the Middle East. Western large-reactor new builds face severe cost and schedule challenges — Hinkley Point C in the UK is projected to cost £46B+ and has faced multiple delays.

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs, <300 MWe): Factory-built, modular, scalable. First commercial SMRs expected ~2030. U.S. leaders: NuScale VOYGR (50–77 MWe, NRC certified), GE Hitachi BWRX-300 (300 MWe), X-energy Xe-100 (80 MWe HTGR), TerraPower Natrium (345 MWe sodium-cooled fast reactor). Target markets: AI data centers, industrial decarbonization, remote/island grids, defense installations.

Micro Reactors (<10 MWe): Truly portable, truck-transportable reactors for remote communities, military forward operating bases, and industrial applications. Oklo Aurora (1.5 MWe), BWXT BANR, Nano Nuclear. DOE is a major customer. Construction timelines: months, not decades. Most still pre-commercial but Oklo broke ground September 2025.

Financial Sponsors & Uranium Supply

Nuclear project finance is a specialized field. Large conventional reactors are primarily financed by regulated utilities (recovering costs through rate base) or government-backed entities. ANATOMY OF A NUCLEAR POWER PLANT

A nuclear power plant converts the energy released by controlled nuclear fission into electricity. Understanding its physical components — from the reactor core to the turbine hall — is essential for evaluating technology risk, capital cost, and operational complexity.

Component

Key Specs

Strategic Significance

Reactor Core

Fuel assemblies (UO₂ rods); neutron moderator (water or graphite); control rods

The heart of the plant. Fission of U-235 releases heat. Control rods absorb neutrons to regulate reaction rate. Passive safety (no active cooling needed in shutdown) is the key SMR design innovation.

Fuel Assemblies

LEU (3–5% U-235) or HALEU (5–20%); zircalloy cladding

Replaced every 18–24 months in conventional reactors. HALEU-fueled SMRs (X-energy, TerraPower) face supply chain bottleneck — Russia is the current dominant HALEU supplier.

Primary Coolant Loop

Pressurized water (~315°C, ~155 bar) or sodium (fast reactors)

Carries heat from reactor core. Pressurized to prevent boiling. Primary loop is radioactive and contained within containment building. Sodium-cooled reactors (TerraPower Natrium) operate at atmospheric pressure — inherently safer.

Steam Generator / Heat Exchanger

Transfers heat from primary to secondary loop

Separates radioactive primary water from non-radioactive steam. Critical maintenance item. In SMRs, often integral to reactor vessel (removing major failure point).

Turbine & Generator

Conventional steam turbine; 33–37% thermal efficiency

Converts steam to electricity. Identical to coal or gas turbine hall. Nuclear's thermal efficiency is lower than modern gas turbines but it runs 91%+ capacity factor vs. 50% for gas.

Containment Structure

Reinforced concrete + steel liner; designed to withstand internal explosion

The defining safety feature of light-water reactors. Prevents radioactive release. TMI and Fukushima containment held; Chernobyl had no Western-style containment. SMRs use underground or submerged containment for enhanced protection.

Spent Fuel Pool

Water-filled pool adjacent to reactor; minimum 5 years before dry cask

The waste management gap: no permanent repository exists globally. U.S. has 90,000 tonnes of spent fuel in on-site storage. Dry cask storage (10+ year lifespan) is the interim solution.

Emergency Core Cooling System

Passive or active water injection; backup power systems

Post-Fukushima: passive safety (gravity-fed cooling without pumps or power) is the standard for new reactor designs. GE Hitachi BWRX-300 and NuScale VOYGR both rely on passive cooling.

How it works: Neutrons strike U-235 atoms in the fuel, splitting them (fission) and releasing energy as heat plus additional neutrons that trigger further fissions (chain reaction). The heat boils water (directly or via heat exchanger) to produce steam that drives a conventional turbine-generator. Nuclear produces ~18–19% of U.S. electricity from just 5% of its power plants — reflecting the extraordinary energy density of nuclear fuel: one uranium fuel pellet (the size of a fingertip) contains as much energy as 17,000 cubic feet of natural gas.

GLOBAL GEOGRAPHY & COUNTRY LEADERS

United States:  The world's largest nuclear fleet by installed capacity (98.4 GW, 96 reactors). Nuclear provides ~18–19% of U.S. electricity — and is the largest source of U.S. carbon-free electricity. The fleet is aging (average 44 years) but achieving record performance (91–93% capacity factors). The AI-driven procurement wave — 9.7+ GW of tech company nuclear deals — is the most significant demand signal for U.S. nuclear since the 1970s. DOE's UPRISE initiative targets +5 GW by 2029 through uprates and restarts.

China:  The world's most active nuclear construction market. ~25 reactors under construction (as of 2026), representing nearly half of all global nuclear construction. China plans to surpass the U.S. as the world's largest nuclear fleet by 2030 (IEA APS scenario). Domestic designs (Hualong One, CAP1000) are increasingly competitive with Western technology. China's nuclear supply chain is largely self-sufficient — from mining (Kazakhstan partnerships) through enrichment, fuel fabrication, and reactor construction.

France:  The most nuclear-intensive large economy globally — nuclear provides ~75% of French electricity. EDF operates 56 reactors. France is aggressively extending reactor lifetimes (from 40 to 50–60 years) and building new EPRs (Flamanville, delayed for years; Hinkley Point C in the UK). France reversed its nuclear phase-out in 2022, now targeting 14 new reactors by 2050 plus SMR development (Nuward).

South Korea:  Builds nuclear faster and cheaper than any other Western-aligned nation. The APR-1400 design — 4 units built in UAE's Barakah plant (first Arab nuclear power plant) on time and on budget — is the benchmark for advanced nuclear construction execution. Korea is competing aggressively for new projects in Czech Republic, UK, Poland, and elsewhere. KEPCO's track record is the strongest evidence that large nuclear can be built predictably in the modern era.

United Kingdom:  Committed to 24 GW of nuclear by 2050 (up from ~6 GW operating today). Hinkley Point C (EDF, 3.2 GW) is under construction but severely over budget (£46B+) and delayed. Sizewell C (EDF + UK government) approved. GE Hitachi BWRX-300 selected for Wylfa site. SMRs are a central pillar of the UK's clean energy strategy. The UK is the most active non-U.S. market for SMR development activity.

India:  Opened its nuclear sector to foreign investment and private capital for the first time via December 2025 legislation, removing the controversial supplier liability law that had blocked Western reactor projects for decades. Rosatom is building 4 additional VVERs at Kudankulam. Westinghouse and GE are now eligible to compete. India has 22 operating reactors and plans for significant expansion.

POLITICS, POLICY & REGULATION

Nuclear occupies a unique political position: it has strong bipartisan support in the U.S. (as baseload clean energy and national security asset), significant public skepticism (Chernobyl, Fukushima, TMI), and a regulatory framework built in the 1970s that is still being modernized for 21st-century reactor designs.

Key Policy Levers MBA Candidates Must Know

Policy / Lever

What It Means for Nuclear

Nuclear Production Tax Credit (IRA)

IRA Section 45U: $15/MWh PTC for existing nuclear plants in the U.S. through 2032. Estimated to save ~$1.3B annually for operators like Constellation and Vistra. OBBBA preserved nuclear PTCs with phase-out beginning 2034 — far more favorable than solar/wind.

ADVANCE Act (2024)

Accelerating Deployment of Versatile, Advanced Nuclear for Clean Energy Act: bipartisan legislation streamlining NRC licensing for advanced reactors, reducing fees, and creating incentives for NRC staff to approve SMRs faster. A landmark modernization of the U.S. nuclear regulatory framework.

DOE Loan Program Office (LPO)

$1B loan guarantee for TMI restart (Crane Clean Energy Center, Nov 2025). LPO has $40B+ in nuclear lending authority under IRA. Palisades (Holtec) also received DOE loan commitment. Government-backed financing is critical for first-of-a-kind nuclear projects.

HALEU Supply Chain

High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (5–20%) is required by most advanced SMR designs. Russia's Rosatom is the dominant HALEU supplier. The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act (2024) bans Russian uranium imports from 2028. DOE awarded $150M+ to Centrus Energy to build U.S. HALEU capacity — a critical national security supply chain initiative.

NRC Licensing Reform

NRC Part 53: new licensing framework for advanced reactors (non-LWR designs). Previously, all reactors had to prove safety relative to existing LWRs — disadvantaging novel designs. Part 53 creates a technology-neutral pathway. Oklo's Aurora license under review 2025–2026.

IAEA & Non-Proliferation

International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safeguards govern uranium enrichment and fuel cycle activities globally. Countries receiving nuclear technology must sign safeguards agreements. Geopolitical tensions (Russia, China, Iran) complicate the international nuclear order. U.S. 123 Agreements govern civil nuclear cooperation with partner nations.

State-Level Support

Many U.S. states have nuclear-specific policies: Illinois enacted legislation keeping Byron and Dresden plants open (2021); New York has zero-emission credit program for nuclear; Ohio provided nuclear support payments. State-level advocacy is increasingly important as the federal regulatory environment modernizes.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: AI Data Centers as the Anchor Tenant for Nuclear  AI Data Centers as the Anchor Tenant for Nuclear

The most transformative development in nuclear energy in decades is not a new reactor design — it's a new customer class. AI data centers need 24/7 carbon-free baseload power at gigawatt scale, and nuclear is the only zero-carbon technology that can deliver it without storage. As of May 2026, 13 announced nuclear deals totaling 9.7+ GW have been signed by Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta. Analysts see this as the beginning of a structural shift in how tech companies procure power — moving from solar PPAs to nuclear PPAs as the "firm" component of their 24/7 clean energy strategy. Fortune describes this as a "tipping point" moment for next-generation nuclear.

Trend 2: SMRs: From Design to Deployment  SMRs: From Design to Deployment

Small Modular Reactors are moving from concept to construction. GE Hitachi's BWRX-300 — selected by Ontario Power Generation, TVA, and the UK's Wylfa site — is the most advanced near-term deployable SMR. TerraPower's Natrium cleared its NRC final safety evaluation in December 2025. Kairos Power's Hermes demonstration reactor at Oak Ridge is under construction. Oklo broke ground in September 2025. The IEA projects SMR capacity reaching 80 GW by 2040. The key question is whether factory-built modules can actually deliver the promised cost reductions — or whether first-of-a-kind construction costs will disappoint as they have for conventional reactors.

Trend 3: Fleet Restarts and Life Extensions  Fleet Restarts and Life Extensions

Rather than waiting for new nuclear builds, the fastest path to additional nuclear capacity is restarting recently retired plants and extending the life of operating ones. The Crane Clean Energy Center (TMI Unit 1) is the flagship — a mothballed reactor being brought back to life for a single tech customer. Holtec's Palisades restart in Michigan (300 MW) represents a similar model. The Nuclear Energy Institute's 2025 survey shows industry planning for 23.4 GWe of new generation through 2039 — 50%+ more than prior years. License renewals for 80-year operation (from the standard 60) are now under NRC review for multiple plants.

Trend 4: The HALEU Bottleneck  The HALEU Bottleneck

High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU, 5–20% enrichment) is required by most advanced SMR designs, including X-energy's Xe-100, TerraPower's Natrium, and Kairos Power's KP-FHR. Russia's Rosatom currently dominates HALEU supply — the same geopolitical vulnerability that plagued Europe's natural gas dependence. The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act (2024) bans Russian uranium from 2028. DOE has contracted with Centrus Energy to build domestic HALEU capacity, but the timeline is tight. HALEU availability is the single biggest non-regulatory bottleneck for advanced SMR deployment in the U.S.

Trend 5: Co-Location: Reactors on Data Center Campuses  Co-Location: Reactors on Data Center Campuses

The most radical emerging model is placing reactors directly on data center campuses — eliminating transmission losses, bypassing grid interconnection queues, and delivering dedicated power behind the meter. Amazon's $20B+ Susquehanna AI campus in Pennsylvania is designed around nuclear co-location. Oklo and RPower are specifically partnering to enable SMR-powered data centers. This model bypasses the grid entirely — similar to how early industrial factories built their own power plants. If regulators permit co-location at scale, it could accelerate SMR deployment by providing an alternative to utility grid integration.

Trend 6: Nuclear Fusion: The Long Game  Nuclear Fusion: The Long Game

Nuclear fusion — the same reaction that powers the sun — produces vastly more energy than fission with no long-lived radioactive waste and essentially unlimited fuel (deuterium from seawater). The December 2022 NIF ignition (energy output exceeded input for the first time in history) was a scientific milestone. Private fusion companies — Commonwealth Fusion Systems (MIT spinout), TAE Technologies, Helion Energy (Microsoft signed a PPA in 2023) — have raised $5B+ combined. Commercial fusion is realistically a 2035–2050 prospect rather than a 2030s technology, but the investment momentum is significant and the talent attracted to the field is extraordinary.

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

Nuclear finance is among the most complex in energy — combining regulated utility economics, government loan guarantees, long-term PPAs, uranium commodity exposure, and emerging venture capital for SMR startups.

Project Finance (Conventional Reactors): Project Finance (Conventional Reactors): Large conventional nuclear is financed by regulated utilities (rate-base recovery) or government-backed entities. Capital costs: $5,000–$15,000/kW for Western large reactors. Revenue from capacity markets + energy markets + nuclear PTCs ($15/MWh under IRA). DOE LPO provides loan guarantees for first-of-a-kind projects. South Korea's KEPCO demonstrates that on-budget nuclear is possible — at $3,000–$4,000/kW — through serial construction and standardized design.

SMR Finance: SMR Finance: The new model. Tech company PPAs (20-year, investment-grade counterparties like Microsoft and Google) are the revenue anchor enabling project finance for SMRs. Infrastructure funds (Brookfield/Westinghouse) are providing equity. DOE LPO loan guarantees cover construction risk. SMR capital costs are targeted at $5,000–$8,000/kW for first-of-a-kind, with series production potentially reaching $3,000–$5,000/kW — but these projections have not been validated by actual construction.

Public Market Opportunities: Public Market Opportunities: Uranium equities (Cameco CCJ, Kazatomprom KAP), nuclear utilities (Constellation CEG, Vistra VST), and SMR pure-plays (NuScale SMR, Oklo OKLO) have all attracted significant investor interest. The Global X Uranium ETF (URA) and Sprott Uranium Miners ETF (URNM) provide uranium exposure. Nuclear utilities have outperformed the S&P significantly since 2023 on the AI power demand thesis.

Venture Capital: Venture Capital: $2.5B+ raised by SMR and advanced nuclear companies in 2025–2026 (TerraPower, X-energy, Kairos Power, Commonwealth Fusion Systems, Oklo, Helion). Breakthrough Energy Ventures (Gates), Prelude Capital, and strategic investors (Amazon, Google, Microsoft) are the most active. Nuclear VC requires patient capital — 10–15 year development cycles — and tolerance for regulatory risk.

Key financial concepts: capacity factor, LCOE for nuclear ($50–90/MWh for existing fleet; $80–120/MWh for new large; $100–150/MWh for first SMRs), levelized avoided cost of energy (LACE), nuclear PTCs, DOE loan guarantees, uranium spot price dynamics, and decommissioning trust fund accounting.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

Nuclear is at a genuine inflection point — the first since before Chernobyl. The combination of AI-driven power demand, climate commitments, and SMR technology maturation has created conditions for a sustained revival. The fundamental physics of nuclear — extraordinary energy density, 90%+ capacity factors, zero-carbon operation — make it uniquely valuable in a world building toward 24/7 carbon-free power systems.

Base Case: Base Case: U.S. adds 5–10 GW of nuclear capacity by 2030 through fleet uprates, restarts (TMI, Palisades), and first SMR deployments. Global nuclear generation reaches record levels annually through 2030. First commercial SMRs (BWRX-300, Natrium) commissioned 2030–2032. IEA low case: global capacity reaches 561 GW by 2050.

Bull Case: Bull Case: SMR factory production achieves cost targets ($3,000–5,000/kW); Big Tech nuclear deals catalyze 20+ GW of U.S. SMR contracts by 2032; India's market opening attracts 10+ GW of Westinghouse and GE orders; nuclear fusion milestone achieved by 2035. IEA high case: global capacity reaches 992 GW by 2050.

Bear Case: Bear Case: First-of-a-kind SMR construction costs disappoint (echoing Vogtle); HALEU supply chain fails to scale; NRC licensing bottlenecks persist; public opposition to reactor siting delays projects. Nuclear growth limited to existing fleet extensions and Chinese domestic builds. TMI restart delayed or cancelled.

The one certainty: nuclear energy's physics have not changed. The energy density of uranium, the reliability of the existing fleet, and the capacity factor advantage over all other clean energy sources are immutable. What has changed is the demand signal — AI has created a customer willing to pay a premium for 24/7 carbon-free power — and the policy environment — the ADVANCE Act, IRA PTCs, and DOE loan guarantees have created the most supportive U.S. nuclear policy environment in 50 years.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"Nuclear is the only zero-carbon energy technology that delivers 24/7 baseload power without storage — and that's exactly what AI data centers need. Microsoft didn't pay $16 billion to restart Three Mile Island because it was the cheapest option. It did it because nuclear is the only technology that can deliver firm, 24/7 clean power at the scale and reliability that AI training clusters require."

"The Vogtle debacle — $14B budget, $35B actual cost, 7 years late — is the reason SMRs exist. If you can't build large nuclear predictably in Western markets, you need a reactor design that can be factory-built, serially produced, and deployed in 3–5 years instead of 15. That's the SMR thesis — and GE Hitachi's BWRX-300 and TerraPower's Natrium are the first real tests of whether it works in practice."

"The HALEU problem is the nuclear equivalent of the FEOC problem in batteries. Russia is the dominant supplier of the high-enriched uranium that most advanced SMRs need — and the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act bans that supply from 2028. You can design the most elegant SMR in the world, but if you can't fuel it, you can't run it. Centrus Energy and DOE's domestic HALEU program are the critical path for the entire advanced nuclear industry."

"The South Korea model is the most underappreciated data point in nuclear finance. KEPCO built four APR-1400 reactors in the UAE on time and on budget at $3,000–4,000/kW — roughly one-quarter the cost of Vogtle. The technology to build nuclear predictably exists. The question is whether the U.S. and Europe can rebuild the construction expertise and supply chains that were allowed to atrophy for 40 years."
