TRANSMISSION & GRID INFRASTRUCTURE

MBA Industry Primer | Energy & Infrastructure

Columbia Business School | April 2026

INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Transmission and grid infrastructure is the foundational layer of the energy economy — every solar panel, wind turbine, nuclear plant, and AI data center depends on a functional grid to deliver its value. For MBA candidates, the grid is simultaneously the most underappreciated and most strategically important asset in the energy transition: it is the bottleneck that prevents renewable energy from connecting, the reason AI data centers can't find power, and the site of a $470 billion annual investment cycle that is projected to nearly double by 2030. Understanding it means understanding how electricity moves from generator to consumer, why the U.S. interconnection queue has over 2,000 GW of projects waiting years for grid access, and why "speed to power" has become the defining competitive advantage in 21st-century energy infrastructure.

HISTORY & ORIGINS

The modern electric grid traces its origins to the "War of Currents" between Thomas Edison (DC) and Nikola Tesla/George Westinghouse (AC) in the late 1880s. Edison's direct current (DC) technology limited transmission to short distances; Tesla's alternating current (AC) could be stepped up to high voltages for long-distance transmission, then stepped down for safe household use. AC won, and the transformer — the device that changes voltage — made the modern interconnected grid possible. The U.S. grid was built incrementally over the 20th century as electrification expanded from cities to rural areas, driven by investor-owned utilities, municipal utilities, and rural electric cooperatives under state regulation.

1930s–1970s: The Regulated Monopoly Era.  The Federal Power Act (1935) established FERC's predecessor and created federal oversight of interstate wholesale electricity. The Rural Electrification Act (1936) brought power to 90% of American farms within a decade. Utilities operated as vertically integrated regulated monopolies — owning generation, transmission, and distribution within their service territories. The grid was designed around large, centralized power plants (coal, nuclear) dispatching electricity to passive consumers. Interconnections between regional grids (Eastern, Western, Texas/ERCOT) were built for reliability, not commercial trading.

1978–2000: Deregulation and Open Access.  The Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA, 1978) required utilities to purchase power from qualifying small generators, creating the independent power producer model. FERC Orders 888 and 889 (1996) mandated open access to transmission networks — requiring utilities to allow competitors to use their wires on non-discriminatory terms. Regional Transmission Organizations (RTOs) and Independent System Operators (ISOs) emerged to operate transmission networks independently of generation interests: PJM, MISO, CAISO, SPP, NYISO, ISO-NE, ERCOT. Wholesale electricity markets were created.

2000–2022: Renewable Integration and the Interconnection Queue Crisis.  The renewable energy boom — solar and wind scaling from marginal to dominant new capacity sources — created an interconnection queue crisis that existing grid rules could not handle. Projects submitted interconnection requests years before construction-ready, clogging queues. Studies took years; costs were uncertain; projects withdrew in large numbers. By 2022, the U.S. interconnection queue had 1,400+ GW awaiting connection. FERC Order 2023 (2023) attempted to reform the queue — moving to cluster studies and first-ready, first-served principles — but implementation has been slow.

2023–2026: The AI Demand Shock.  The generative AI revolution created an unprecedented new category of grid challenge: massive, fast-growing electricity loads (AI data centers) seeking connections to grids that had never contemplated demand queues of this scale. Data centers caused a $9.3B price increase in PJM's 2025–26 capacity market. Northern Virginia ran out of available power for new connections. Texas passed Senate Bill 6 (2025) to manage large load interconnection in ERCOT. NERC revised its 10-year U.S. load growth forecast from 6.1% to 11.6%. The term "speed to power" entered the data center industry lexicon as the defining competitive variable. BloombergNEF projected global grid investment crossing $470B for the first time in 2025.

MARKET STRUCTURE & VALUE CHAIN

The electricity grid is organized hierarchically from high-voltage transmission (interstate) through sub-transmission to distribution (local delivery). Each layer has different regulatory structures, technology requirements, investment dynamics, and ownership models.

The Grid Infrastructure Value Chain

1. Generation Interconnection

The process by which a new power plant (or large load like a data center) connects to the transmission grid. FERC-regulated in the U.S. (Orders 2003, 2023). Regional queue managed by RTOs/ISOs. Study process takes 2–5 years; costs uncertain until late in study. The primary bottleneck in U.S. renewable energy deployment.

2. Substation & Transformer Infrastructure

High-voltage substations step down transmission voltage to sub-transmission levels. Power transformers (500 kV, 345 kV, 230 kV) are long lead-time, specialty items — 12–24 months from order to delivery. ABB (Hitachi Energy), Siemens Energy, GE Vernova dominate supply. U.S. domestic transformer manufacturing capacity is insufficient for current demand growth.

3. High-Voltage Transmission (HV/EHV)

345 kV to 765 kV lines carrying power hundreds of miles. FERC-regulated; state siting authority varies. Transmission line construction: $1–5M/mile for overhead; $10–50M/mile for underground. Key developers: ATC (American Transmission Co.), GridLiance, Transource. IRA and Grid Infrastructure Act funding supports new transmission.

4. HVDC (High-Voltage Direct Current)

Enables efficient long-distance transmission (>300 miles), asynchronous grid interconnection (connecting Eastern/Western grids), and offshore wind connection. Technology advantage: losses only 3% per 1,000 km vs. 5–8% for HVAC. ABB (Hitachi Energy) and Siemens Energy dominate HVDC. Key projects: SunZia (New Mexico to Arizona, 550 MW HVDC), Grain Belt Express, TransWest Express.

5. Grid Management Software & SCADA

Energy Management Systems (EMS) and SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) run real-time grid operations. Advanced grid analytics, AI-driven demand forecasting, and automated protection systems. GE Vernova (GridOS), Siemens, Oracle (OCI for utilities), ABB. AI integration transforming grid control — predicting load, optimizing dispatch, detecting faults faster.

6. Distribution System (Medium/Low Voltage)

Distribution lines (4–35 kV) deliver power to homes and businesses. Regulated utility-owned. The "last mile" of electricity delivery. Increasingly bidirectional as distributed energy resources (solar, storage, EVs) inject power onto distribution networks. Grid modernization (smart meters, automated distribution, DER management systems) is the primary distribution investment driver.

7. Demand Response & Grid Edge

Using controllable loads (industrial motors, EV chargers, HVAC, water heaters) as grid resources — curtailing or shifting demand to balance supply. FERC Order 2222 (2020) opened wholesale markets to distributed energy resources. Itron, Enel X, Voltus, AutoGrid aggregate demand response capacity. Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) — aggregating millions of small devices — are the emerging grid edge business model.

Three market segments define grid infrastructure investment:

Transmission (Highest Growth): High-voltage lines, substations, HVDC links. Growing at ~16% CAGR through 2027 (BloombergNEF). Driven by renewable energy interconnection, AI data center demand, and long-distance transmission for geographic balancing. Transmission investment is projected to surpass distribution investment globally before 2030.

Distribution Modernization: Smart meters (AMI), automated switching, distribution automation, DER integration platforms. Growing at ~9% CAGR. Every utility has a grid modernization capital plan. The proliferation of rooftop solar, EVs, and home batteries is forcing distribution networks to handle two-way power flows they were never designed for.

Grid Services & Software: Energy management systems, protection and control systems, demand response platforms, grid analytics. Faster-growing than hardware but smaller absolute market. GE Vernova's GridOS, Siemens Gridscale X, and a wave of VC-backed startups (SparkCognition, eSmart Systems, AutoGrid) are competing in this space.

Financial Sponsors & Infrastructure Investors

Regulated transmission assets are among the most attractive in all of infrastructure finance: stable, inflation-linked returns (typically allowed ROE of 10–12% in the U.S.), long asset lives (40–60 years), and essential service provision. Transmission-focused companies (ATC, ITC Holdings, GridLiance) and portions of vertically integrated utilities are prime targets for infrastructure fund investment. Macquarie, Brookfield, KKR, and AMP Capital have all owned regulated transmission assets. FERC's base ROE determinations are the key regulatory variable — FERC sets the return on equity utilities are allowed to earn on transmission investment. The Build Act and Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) provide $65B for grid infrastructure, including grants for new transmission and grid modernization.

ANATOMY OF THE ELECTRIC GRID

The electric grid is a system of interconnected components that generates, transmits, and distributes electricity from power plants to end users in real time — at scales ranging from a single home's 240V outlet to 765,000-volt interstate transmission lines.

Component

Key Specs

Strategic Significance

Generation Tie-Lines

138–345 kV lines connecting generators to grid; sized to rated capacity

The interconnection point where a renewable farm or nuclear plant connects to the transmission grid. Interconnection study determines required upgrades and cost allocation — often hundreds of millions of dollars. The primary reason renewable projects are delayed.

Transmission Lines (HV)

345 kV – 765 kV; overhead (steel towers) or underground (cross-linked polyethylene cable)

Backbone of the grid. Overhead AC lines: $1–4M/mile. Underground: $10–50M/mile (10× more expensive). 765 kV EHV lines can carry 2,000–5,000 MW over long distances — enabling geographic diversification of renewable energy.

High-Voltage Transformers (HVT)

500 kV, 345 kV, 230 kV; 50–1,000 MVA capacity; custom-manufactured

Single most critical piece of grid equipment. Lead times: 12–24 months. No domestic U.S. manufacturing for the largest units (500 kV, 500+ MVA) — imported from ABB, Siemens, GE. A transformer failure can cause regional blackouts. Transformer backlog is a grid vulnerability.

Substations

Switching, protection, voltage transformation; 4–40 acres; $50–500M each

Where transmission meets distribution. High-voltage switchgear, circuit breakers, current transformers, surge arrestors. Data center load growth is forcing substation upgrades across PJM and ERCOT. Substation automation and digital twins are the primary modernization investment.

HVDC Converter Stations

Rectifier (AC→DC) and inverter (DC→AC); ±500 kV to ±800 kV

Enables long-distance transmission with low losses; connects asynchronous grids (e.g., Eastern-Western U.S. connection); essential for offshore wind. Converter stations cost $200–500M each. ABB Hitachi Energy and Siemens Energy dominate globally.

Distribution Transformers

10–2,500 kVA; on poles or in underground vaults; 2–5 year replacement cycle

The final voltage step-down to household level (120/240V). U.S. has 50M+ distribution transformers. The EV and rooftop solar boom is overloading distribution transformers faster than utilities can replace them. Lead times surged to 1–2 years post-pandemic.

Smart Meters & Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI)

Bi-directional communication; interval data; remote disconnect/reconnect

The data layer of the modern grid. 120M+ U.S. smart meters deployed. Enable real-time pricing signals, demand response, EV charging optimization, and distribution grid situational awareness. Itron, Landis+Gyr, Honeywell are the primary AMI vendors.

How the grid works: Electricity is generated at power plants (synchronous generators spinning at 60 Hz in North America) and stepped up to high voltage by generators' step-up transformers for efficient transmission. High-voltage AC lines carry power hundreds of miles with acceptable losses (~5%). At load centers, substations step voltage down through transmission and sub-transmission levels to distribution voltage (4–35 kV). Distribution lines carry power to neighborhood transformers that step it down further to 120/240V for homes and businesses. The entire system operates in real time — electricity cannot be meaningfully stored at grid scale (beyond available battery storage) and must be continuously balanced between supply and demand. Grid operators (RTO/ISO or utility) dispatch generators second-by-second to maintain 60 Hz frequency. Deviation from 60 Hz by more than a small margin causes equipment failures and blackouts.

GLOBAL GEOGRAPHY & COUNTRY LEADERS

United States:  The largest single-country grid investment market ($115B in 2025, 25% of global total) but also the most fragmented grid governance. The U.S. grid is divided into three major synchronous interconnections (Eastern, Western, Texas/ERCOT), 7 RTOs/ISOs, hundreds of investor-owned utilities, and thousands of municipal utilities and co-ops. This fragmentation — combined with FERC jurisdiction over interstate transmission and state jurisdiction over intrastate transmission and distribution — creates a complex multi-stakeholder approval process that typically takes 7–15 years for a major new interstate transmission line. NERC revised load growth from 6.1% to 11.6% over the decade — the highest upward revision in NERC history.

China:  The world's largest electricity grid by both capacity and investment. State Grid Corporation of China (SGCC) and China Southern Power Grid operate essentially without the regulatory fragmentation that constrains U.S. grid development. China deploys ultra-high voltage (UHV) DC lines at ±800 kV or ±1,100 kV — the highest voltage in the world — carrying 10,000+ MW over 3,000+ km from renewable-rich western provinces to demand centers in the east. China can approve, permit, and build a 3,000-km UHV line in 3–4 years — compared to 10–15 years for a major U.S. interstate line. Chinese grid investment (~$95B/year) rivals U.S. investment in scale.

Europe:  The EU targets a fully interconnected European grid capable of accommodating 100% renewable electricity by 2050. The key challenge: national grid operators (National Grid UK, Elia Belgium, RTE France, Amprion/50Hertz Germany, Terna Italy) coordinate through ENTSO-E but each requires national regulatory approval. The North Sea Grid — meshed HVDC connections enabling offshore wind from the UK, Denmark, Netherlands, Germany, and Norway to supply multiple countries — is the flagship project. Baltic offshore wind integration is equally important.

India:  One of the world's most ambitious grid expansion programs. Power Grid Corporation of India (PGCIL) is investing $25B+ in transmission infrastructure to support 500 GW of renewables by 2030. India's transmission network is growing rapidly but reliability challenges persist — rural areas experience frequent outages. HVDC links connecting renewable-rich western states (Rajasthan, Gujarat) to demand centers (Mumbai, Delhi) are the primary investment priority.

POLITICS, POLICY & REGULATION

Grid infrastructure is perhaps the most regulated segment of the energy sector — every transmission line, substation, and rate requires regulatory approval at federal or state level. Understanding the regulatory framework is essential for infrastructure investing and utility strategy roles.

Key Policy Levers MBA Candidates Must Know

Policy / Lever

What It Means for Grid Infrastructure

FERC Order 2023 (2023)

Reformed U.S. interconnection queue with cluster study approach, first-ready first-served criteria, and study deposit requirements. Largest reform since Order 2003. Implementation ongoing — RTOs still processing 2,000+ GW backlog. Insufficient for AI-era demand; further reform expected.

FERC Order 1920 (2024)

Long-term regional transmission planning reform: requires utilities to perform 20-year forward-looking transmission planning, including scenarios for high renewable integration. Most significant transmission planning reform in 15 years. Expected to unlock $100B+ in new transmission investment.

DOE Speed to Power Initiative

DOE RFI (September 2025) seeking to identify large-scale generation and transmission projects that can deliver 3+ GW increments to support AI/data center demand. Coordination, technical assistance, and financial support available. Reflects the urgency of the power bottleneck.

Grid Infrastructure Act (IIJA, 2021)

$65B for grid infrastructure: $2.5B for grid resilience grants, $2.5B for Transmission Facilitation Program (federal transmission investment), $10.5B for grid resilience and innovation partnerships, $3B for Smart Grid Investment Matching Grant Program.

Transmission Siting Reform

Federal vs. state authority to site interstate transmission is the most contested grid policy debate. FERC has limited siting authority (backstop under Section 216); most transmission siting requires state approval. Biden DOE issued National Interest Electric Transmission Corridor designations; the Trump administration approach is evolving.

NERC Reliability Standards

North American Electric Reliability Corporation sets mandatory reliability standards (TPL, FAC, BAL series) for grid operators and utilities. NERC's 2025 assessment revised U.S. 10-year load growth from 6.1% to 11.6% — triggering reliability concern given current transmission constraints.

Texas SB 6 (2025)

Texas legislation redefining large load interconnection in ERCOT for facilities >75 MW (primarily data centers): mandatory interconnection study fee, site control requirement, financial commitment to transmission upgrade costs. First state legislation specifically addressing data center grid impact.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: The $470B Annual Investment Cycle and the Utility Super-Cycle  The $470B Annual Investment Cycle and the Utility Super-Cycle

Global grid investment crossed $470B in 2025 for the first time in history, and Morningstar DBRS characterizes U.S. electric utilities as entering an investment "super-cycle" comparable to the rural electrification era of the 1930s. Investor-owned utilities are projected to spend $1.1 trillion on grid infrastructure from 2025 to 2029. BloombergNEF projects transmission investment growing at 16% CAGR through 2027. The driver is not just renewables — it's the simultaneous convergence of renewable integration, AI data center demand, electric vehicle load growth, industrial electrification, and decades of deferred grid maintenance. This creates a multi-decade capital deployment opportunity that is already reshaping utility equity valuations.

Trend 2: HVDC: The Technology Enabling Long-Distance Clean Energy  HVDC: The Technology Enabling Long-Distance Clean Energy

High-Voltage Direct Current technology is experiencing its most significant deployment wave since the first HVDC links were built in the 1950s. HVDC enables three things impossible or impractical with AC: (1) efficient transmission over 300+ miles with minimal losses, (2) connection of asynchronous grids (Eastern-Western U.S. connection), and (3) offshore wind collection from multiple farms across different countries. The U.S. has a limited HVDC network today, but projects like SunZia (550 MW, New Mexico-Arizona), Grain Belt Express (5,000 MW, Kansas to Illinois), TransWest Express (3,000 MW, Wyoming to California), and SOO Green (2,100 MW, Iowa to Illinois) are building the backbone of a new HVDC network. In Europe, the North Sea Wind Power Hub concept envisions an artificial energy island with HVDC connections to six countries.

Trend 3: Behind-the-Meter and On-Site Generation: Bypassing the Grid  Behind-the-Meter and On-Site Generation: Bypassing the Grid

As grid interconnection times stretch to 5–7 years, a growing number of data center developers are exploring "behind-the-meter" solutions — generating power on-site to bypass the public grid entirely. Natural gas peakers, micro-nuclear reactors (Oklo), fuel cells (Bloom Energy, which reported 60%+ data center revenue growth guidance), and large-scale BESS are being deployed directly on data center campuses. Bloom Energy's 2026 Data Center Power Report found that expectations of on-site power deployment are growing among data center developers. Ireland and Texas have implemented "bring your own power" mandates, formalizing this trend.

Trend 4: AI for Grid Operations: Digital Twins and Predictive Analytics  AI for Grid Operations: Digital Twins and Predictive Analytics

AI is transforming how grids are operated and maintained. Digital twins of transmission infrastructure enable real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance — identifying equipment failure risk before outages occur. AI-powered demand forecasting improves day-ahead and real-time dispatch, reducing spinning reserve costs. Automated protection systems can isolate faults in milliseconds, reducing outage impact. GE Vernova's GridOS, Siemens' Gridscale X, and a wave of startups (Utilidata, SparkCognition, AutoGrid) are competing for the AI-powered grid management market. IBM and Oracle are providing cloud infrastructure for utility data platforms.

Trend 5: Virtual Power Plants: The Grid's New Demand Management Tool  Virtual Power Plants: The Grid's New Demand Management Tool

Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) aggregate millions of distributed energy resources — rooftop solar, home batteries, smart thermostats, EV chargers, water heaters — into a coordinated grid resource. FERC Order 2222 (2020) opened wholesale electricity markets to aggregated distributed resources, giving VPPs access to the same capacity and ancillary service markets as traditional power plants. Tesla's Virtual Power Plant (2,000+ Powerwall customers in California), AutoGrid's FlexPower platform, and Voltus's demand response aggregation demonstrate the model at scale. VPPs are particularly valuable during grid emergencies when traditional demand response is most needed.

Trend 6: Transmission Siting Reform: The Decade’s Most Important Grid Policy Fight  Transmission Siting Reform: The Decade's Most Important Grid Policy Fight

Building a major new interstate transmission line in the U.S. takes 10–15 years — roughly half the time from development to commissioning. The primary reason: transmission lines cross multiple states, each requiring separate siting approval under state public utility commission authority. FERC's backstop siting authority under FPA Section 216 (National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors) is limited and rarely used. The result: renewable energy that could be generated cheaply in high-wind/solar areas (Great Plains, Southwest) cannot cost-effectively reach demand centers (Midwest, Northeast, California) because the transmission lines don't exist. FERC Order 1920's 20-year planning requirement is a step toward solving this — but siting reform requires Congressional action that has repeatedly failed.

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

Grid infrastructure offers MBA candidates exposure to the most stable, long-duration infrastructure asset class in energy — regulated returns, monopoly characteristics, and essential service provision — alongside the fastest-growing capital deployment cycle in U.S. utility history.

Regulated Utility Investing: Regulated Utility Investing: Regulated transmission assets earn FERC-approved returns on equity (currently ~10–12% for most utilities). Regulated utilities are valued on a price-to-book or EV/rate base multiple — reflecting the regulatory compact that allows utilities to earn a return on their invested capital base. Higher capital investment = higher rate base = higher earnings. The current super-cycle makes utilities' capital plans the primary driver of earnings growth. Key metrics: capital expenditure plan, rate base growth, allowed ROE, regulatory lag, and earned ROE vs. allowed.

Infrastructure Funds: Infrastructure Funds: Transmission-focused investment companies (ATC, GridLiance, ITC Holdings) and portions of utilities are attractive infrastructure fund targets. Long-duration (40–60 year asset lives), inflation-linked returns (rate cases typically incorporate inflation), monopoly characteristics, and essential service provision. Typical infrastructure equity IRR targets: 9–12% for regulated transmission; 12–16% for merchant or contracted transmission (competitive projects).

Project Finance (Merchant/Contracted Transmission): Project Finance (Merchant/Contracted Transmission): New transmission lines outside the traditional regulated utility framework — merchant HVDC lines (Grain Belt Express, SunZia) and contracted lines with specific generators — can be project-financed. Revenue: capacity tolling agreements with generators or utilities, or merchant congestion revenue. Construction risk is the primary challenge — unlike generation projects, there is no pre-existing revenue stream during development.

Equipment & Technology Investing: Equipment & Technology Investing: The grid investment super-cycle is creating significant value in equipment companies (GE Vernova, Eaton, ABB/Hitachi Energy, Prysmian) and grid technology companies (grid management software, digital twins, VPP platforms). GE Vernova's NYSE IPO (2023) and its subsequent outperformance reflects the market's recognition of grid investment tailwinds. VC investment in grid-edge technology (demand response, VPPs, grid AI) has surged.

Key financial concepts: rate base, allowed ROE, regulatory compact, rate case (formula rates vs. rate cases), CWIP (Construction Work In Progress), Allowance for Funds Used During Construction (AFUDC), congestion revenue rights, Financial Transmission Rights (FTRs), PJM capacity market mechanics, and FERC transmission tariff structures.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

The grid is facing its most significant transformation since rural electrification in the 1930s — and it must happen faster, at larger scale, and with more complex stakeholder management than any previous grid investment cycle. The scale of investment required is not in question. The constraints are permitting timelines, equipment supply chains, and the regulatory frameworks designed for a simpler era.

Base Case: Base Case: U.S. grid investment sustains at $100B+/year through 2030. Transmission grows faster than distribution. FERC Order 1920 unlocks $100B+ in new long-range transmission. HVDC projects (SunZia, Grain Belt Express, TransWest Express) complete. Interconnection queue reforms reduce average study times from 5 years to 3 years. Global grid investment reaches $700–800B/year by 2030.

Bull Case: Bull Case: Congressional transmission siting reform passes — enabling federal backstop authority for interstate transmission; interconnection queue cleared with aggressive FERC action; domestic transformer manufacturing capacity expanded under IRA Section 48C; battery storage and demand response reduce peak grid stress; global grid investment reaches $1T/year by 2030.

Bear Case: Bear Case: Permitting gridlock continues; transformer supply chain bottlenecks constrain grid expansion pace; political resistance to transmission lines in specific regions (rural landowner opposition, "not in my backyard") blocks key projects; AI investment disappoints and data center demand growth moderates. Grid investment plateaus at $500–600B/year globally.

The one certainty: the grid must grow. Every renewable energy project, every AI data center, every electric vehicle, and every industrial electrification initiative requires a functioning, expanded grid to deliver its value. The grid is not optional infrastructure — it is the foundation on which the entire energy transition rests. The investors, engineers, and policymakers who solve the grid's bottlenecks will have created more value than any other single intervention in the clean energy transition.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"The grid is the energy transition's biggest bottleneck that nobody in finance talks about enough. There's 2,000 GW of renewable energy and storage projects in the U.S. interconnection queue — more than twice the entire current installed U.S. generation fleet. Most will never be built, not because of lack of capital or technology, but because the grid they need to connect to doesn't have space. That's not a technology problem or a finance problem — it's a regulatory and permitting problem that only policy reform can solve."

"BloombergNEF's finding that global grid investment crossed $470B in 2025 — the first time ever — understates the scale of what's coming. U.S. utilities alone are planning to spend $1.1 trillion on grid infrastructure in the next five years. That's more than the entire market cap of most S&P 500 companies deployed in a single infrastructure category. The companies that supply transformers, cables, and grid management software to utilities are some of the best secular growth stories in the market right now."

"The high-voltage transformer market is a case study in supply chain fragility. The U.S. has essentially no domestic manufacturing capacity for the largest transformers — they're imported from Germany, South Korea, and Japan. Lead times are now 18–24 months. A single transformer failure at a major substation can cause a regional blackout affecting millions of people. And we're simultaneously trying to triple the rate of grid expansion. The transformer supply chain is the grid's most acute single-point vulnerability."

"FERC Order 1920's 20-year transmission planning requirement is the most important grid policy action since deregulation. It forces utilities to plan for a future grid, not the current one — and it explicitly requires scenarios with high renewable penetration. If implemented effectively, it should unlock $100B+ in transmission investment that would never be built under current planning frameworks. The question is whether the political will exists to route those lines across state borders when landowner and community opposition materializes."
