INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Water infrastructure is the most essential and most underfunded sector in the global economy. Every human being requires approximately 2 liters of drinking water daily to survive and 50–100 liters for basic sanitation — making water infrastructure the most fundamental of all infrastructure categories. Yet globally, 2.3 billion people live in water-stressed regions, 785 million lack basic drinking water service, and the World Bank estimates a $7 trillion global infrastructure funding gap by 2030. In the United States, the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) gives drinking water infrastructure a C+ and wastewater a D+ in its 2025 Report Card — with a $2.1–2.4 trillion investment need over the next 25 years just for drinking water systems. For MBA candidates, water infrastructure sits at the intersection of regulated utility investing, climate resilience, public health regulation (PFAS, lead service lines), and the emerging water technology sector. Understanding it means understanding why PFAS "forever chemicals" are creating a $100B+ remediation market, why American Water Works and Essential Utilities merged to form a $63B combined utility, how desalination is becoming a strategic national security asset in water-scarce nations, and why water rights — in many U.S. western states — are becoming more valuable than the land itself.

HISTORY & ORIGINS: FROM ROMAN AQUEDUCTS TO SMART WATER SYSTEMS

Water infrastructure is the oldest form of public infrastructure — Roman aqueducts (312 BC) delivered clean water to cities across the Empire using gravity-fed stone channels that still function 2,300 years later. Modern water infrastructure is both the direct descendant of these ancient systems (gravity-fed distribution remains the primary principle) and a technology-intensive industry being transformed by digital monitoring, membrane filtration, and AI-driven management.

The Public Health Revolution (1850s–1940s)

Modern urban water infrastructure emerged directly from the public health crises of 19th century industrialization. London’s Great Stink of 1858 — when the River Thames, serving as both water supply and sewage dump for 2.5 million people, became unbearable in summer heat — triggered Joseph Bazalgette’s construction of London’s intercepting sewer system (1858–1875), one of the greatest engineering achievements of the Victorian era. John Snow’s epidemiological mapping of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak proved that contaminated water caused the disease and established the scientific basis for water treatment.

In the United States, rapid urbanization created parallel crises. The passage of the Public Health Service Act (1912) and the subsequent Safe Drinking Water Act (1974) established federal water quality standards. Chlorination of drinking water (Jersey City, NJ, 1908 — the first U.S. city) was the single most impactful public health intervention in American history, reducing typhoid death rates by 90%+ within two decades. The federal government funded massive water infrastructure expansion through the Works Progress Administration (1930s, New Deal) and the Federal Water Pollution Control Act (1948, predecessor to the Clean Water Act).

The Regulatory Era (1970s–2010s)

The Clean Water Act (1972) and Safe Drinking Water Act (1974) established the modern regulatory framework for U.S. water quality, requiring permits for all wastewater discharges and setting maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for drinking water pollutants. These laws triggered a $500B+ investment in wastewater treatment plant construction and drinking water system upgrades over the following 30 years, funded primarily through federal grants and state revolving funds (SRFs).

The privatization of water utilities became a significant policy debate in the 1990s and 2000s as municipalities struggled with aging infrastructure and budget constraints. France’s Veolia (formerly Vivendi Environnement) and Suez became global water utility operators, taking over management of municipal water systems in Atlanta, Indianapolis, and internationally in Buenos Aires, Manila, and Dar es Salaam. Results were mixed: private operators improved efficiency in some systems but faced political backlash over rate increases, service quality, and the commoditization of a public good in others. The most successful privatization models (England and Wales, where full privatization occurred in 1989 with Ofwat as regulator) have demonstrated that regulation design matters more than ownership structure.

The 21st Century: Aging Systems, Emerging Contaminants, and Climate Stress (2010–Present)

The Flint, Michigan water crisis (2014–2019) was the defining water infrastructure event of the decade: the city's decision to switch water sources to save money led to corrosion of lead pipes, contaminating drinking water for 100,000+ residents, including children whose neurological development was permanently damaged by lead exposure. Flint demonstrated that aging water infrastructure was not just a fiscal problem but an acute public health emergency — and that the systems serving low-income communities were at highest risk. The crisis catalyzed the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law’s $15B commitment to lead service line replacement.

PFAS ("forever chemicals") — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances used in firefighting foams, non-stick cookware, and industrial processes — emerged as the defining water quality challenge of the 2020s. PFAS are detectable in 45%+ of U.S. tap water and in virtually every water system that has been tested. The EPA’s first national PFAS drinking water limits (PFOA: 4 parts per trillion; PFOS: 4 ppt, finalized 2024) are so stringent that virtually every U.S. utility will require treatment upgrades, creating an estimated $80B+ remediation market. 3M’s $10.3B and DuPont’s $1.19B PFAS settlements began flowing to water utilities in 2025, providing partial but insufficient funding for the necessary treatment upgrades.

THE WATER INFRASTRUCTURE VALUE CHAIN

Water infrastructure encompasses two parallel systems: the drinking water system (treatment and distribution of potable water) and the wastewater system (collection and treatment of used water). These systems share infrastructure in some cases (combined sewer overflows) but are fundamentally distinct in function and regulatory framework.

System / Stage

Economics, Technology & Investment Characteristics

Source Water & Watershed Management

The starting point: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). Source water quality determines treatment cost: unprotected watersheds with agricultural runoff require more treatment than protected mountain reservoirs. Groundwater depletion is a critical long-term risk: the Ogallala Aquifer (underlies 8 Great Plains states, irrigates 30% of U.S. crops) has been drawn down 10–25 feet in much of its range since 1950; parts of California's San Joaquin Valley have sunk 8–30 feet due to groundwater extraction (land subsidence). Investment: watershed protection programs ($1–5/acre), aquifer recharge projects, and surface water storage expansion (dams, reservoirs).

Water Treatment (Potable)

Conventional treatment: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection (chlorine, UV, ozone). Advanced treatment: activated carbon (organic compound removal), membrane filtration (ultrafiltration, nanofiltration, reverse osmosis for recycled water), and ion exchange (PFAS removal). PFAS treatment is the dominant capital investment driver for 2025–2031: granular activated carbon (GAC) and ion exchange (IX) are the EPA-approved technologies; a typical medium-sized utility (serving 50,000 connections) faces $20–50M in treatment upgrades. Key players: Xylem (filtration systems), Veolia (treatment operations), Suez Water, Evoqua (now Xylem), AECOM, CDM Smith (engineering and construction).

Transmission & Distribution Network

The highest-capital-cost component: pipes, pumps, pressure zones, storage tanks, and fire hydrants. The U.S. has 2.2 million miles of water distribution pipe, much of it installed in the 1920s–1960s with a design life of 50–75 years — meaning a large fraction is past useful life. Water main breaks occur at a rate of 250,000/year nationally; the average water utility loses 20–30% of its water to leaks before it reaches customers (non-revenue water). Smart meters (Advanced Metering Infrastructure, AMI) and acoustic leak detection are the primary technology investments for network management. Lead service lines (the connection from the main to the house) are the most acutely toxic aging component: 9.2 million nationwide require replacement.

Customer Metering & Billing

Smart metering (AMI) is replacing manual meter reading across U.S. utilities: sensors transmit hourly consumption data, enabling leak detection, demand forecasting, and time-of-use pricing. Xylem, Itron, and Sensus (Xylem subsidiary) are the dominant AMI providers. The data generated by smart metering is the foundation of the "digital water utility" — using AI to optimize operations, predict main breaks, and manage drought-driven demand reduction programs. Revenue protection (reducing non-revenue water from leaks and theft) is the primary ROI driver for AMI investment.

Wastewater Collection (Sewer)

Gravity-fed underground pipe networks (typically separate sanitary sewers plus storm drains; combined sewer systems in older cities mix both). Combined Sewer Overflows (CSOs) — where combined systems overflow during heavy rain, sending untreated sewage into waterways — are the primary Clean Water Act compliance challenge for older U.S. cities (Washington DC, Boston, Chicago). CSO remediation programs cost $1–10B+ per city. Inflow and infiltration (I&I) — groundwater entering sewer pipes through cracks — inflates treatment costs at wastewater plants. CIPP (cured-in-place pipe) lining is the primary rehabilitation technology for aging sewer mains.

Wastewater Treatment

Advanced biological treatment removes nitrogen and phosphorus (nutrients that cause algal blooms and hypoxic dead zones in receiving water bodies); Biological Nutrient Removal (BNR) is now the standard for major facilities. Emerging: membrane bioreactors (MBR) for high-quality effluent; advanced oxidation for micropollutant (pharmaceuticals, PFAS) removal. Resource recovery is the frontier: nutrient recovery (struvite for fertilizer), biogas capture (wastewater plant digester gas can supply 50%+ of facility energy), and water recycling (potable reuse). Orange County Water District’s Groundwater Replenishment System (California) is the world’s largest potable water reuse facility: it purifies wastewater to drinking water standards and recharges the local aquifer.

Desalination

Reverse osmosis (RO) is the dominant desalination technology: seawater is forced under pressure through semi-permeable membranes that reject salt ions. The cost of seawater desalination has fallen dramatically: from $2–3/m³ in 2000 to $0.50–80¢/m³ in the most efficient modern plants. Desalination provides 1% of global freshwater supply but is strategic in water-scarce regions: Israel gets 80% of its drinking water from desalination (Sorek, Ashdod, Hadera plants); Saudi Arabia's Ras Al Khair is the world’s largest seawater desalination plant (1.025 million m³/day); California’s Carlsbad plant (189,000 m³/day) is the largest in the Western Hemisphere. Energy cost (30–40% of operating cost) is the primary barrier; renewable-integrated desalination (solar-powered RO) reduces costs 20–30% and is growing rapidly.

PFAS: THE DEFINING WATER CHALLENGE OF THE DECADE

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a family of 12,000+ synthetic chemicals used in manufacturing since the 1940s. They are called "forever chemicals" because they do not break down in the environment or human body. PFAS are now detected in 45%+ of U.S. tap water, virtually all human blood samples, and the vast majority of water systems that have been tested. The public health implications are severe: PFAS exposure is linked to kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disease, immune system suppression, and developmental effects in children.

The Regulatory Framework

The EPA finalized the first U.S. National Primary Drinking Water Regulation (NPDWR) for PFAS in April 2024, setting maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for six PFAS compounds. PFOA and PFOS (the two most studied, historically used in Teflon and firefighting foam): 4 parts per trillion — among the lowest drinking water standards ever set. The compliance deadline is 2031. This standard is so stringent that virtually every U.S. water system will need to test for and likely treat PFAS contamination. The estimated cost: $800M annually for monitoring, with treatment upgrades ranging from $5M (small system, ion exchange) to $100M+ (large utility, GAC treatment). The Trump administration has confirmed PFAS regulations will move forward, though some limit adjustments are possible.

The Litigation Settlements

3M agreed to pay $10.3B to settle PFAS contamination lawsuits from U.S. water utilities (2023, payments beginning 2024); DuPont, Chemours, and Corteva agreed to pay $1.19B (2023); Tyco International (firefighting foam) and other defendants have made additional settlements. These settlement proceeds — flowing to utilities from summer 2025 — provide partial but clearly insufficient funding for the estimated $80B+ total remediation cost. The settlements have created a unique investment dynamic: utilities with PFAS contamination now have cash to deploy on treatment infrastructure, creating an accelerated procurement cycle for granular activated carbon systems, ion exchange units, and membrane filtration.

The Technology Winners

Granular Activated Carbon (GAC) and Ion Exchange (IX) are the EPA-designated Best Available Technologies for PFAS removal. PFAS destruction technologies (electrochemical oxidation, supercritical water oxidation, sonochemical treatment) are in active R&D and commercial scale-up. Aclarity (Burnt Island Ventures portfolio), 374Water (acquired Aqueous Solutions’ SCWO technology), and several VC-backed companies are developing next-generation PFAS destruction that goes beyond removal to elimination. The PFAS treatment market is projected to grow from $2B (2024) to $15–20B+ by 2031 as compliance deadlines approach.

GLOBAL WATER SCARCITY & CLIMATE NEXUS

Water scarcity — the condition where demand exceeds available supply — is the primary long-term driver of water infrastructure investment globally. Climate change is not creating new water scarcity in most cases; it is amplifying and accelerating existing stress, making previously marginal situations acute.

The Global Water Stress Map

The World Resources Institute’s Aqueduct tool identifies countries facing extreme water stress (using 80%+ of available renewable water annually): Qatar, Israel, Lebanon, Iran, Jordan, Libya, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Eritrea, UAE, San Marino, Bahrain, India (many states), Pakistan, Turkmenistan. The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region is the world’s most water-stressed: most countries have essentially zero renewable freshwater from rain — their water comes exclusively from desalination, fossil aquifers (non-renewable), or the River Nile (shared, contested). In the U.S., the Colorado River Basin serves 40 million people and irrigates 5.5 million acres, but its natural flow is being consumed at 110% of the actual supply — meaning Lake Mead and Lake Powell have been drawing down reserves accumulated over decades. The 2023–2024 partial recovery is a temporary reprieve, not a structural fix.

The Hormuz Crisis and Water Security (2026)  The Hormuz Crisis and Water Security

The February 2026 Strait of Hormuz closure created a secondary water security crisis that most analysts missed: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, and Bahrain produce virtually all of their drinking water from energy-intensive desalination plants. When energy infrastructure in the region was disrupted and oil prices spiked to $117/barrel, the energy cost of desalination increased dramatically. The WEF noted that the Hormuz crisis was disrupting not just oil but “methanol, aluminium, sulphur, and graphite” — all key inputs to water treatment and desalination. The crisis demonstrated that water security and energy security are inseparable for water-scarce nations: a country that produces its drinking water from desalination is only as water-secure as it is energy-secure.

Climate Change and Infrastructure Design  Climate Change and Water Infrastructure Design

The most technically demanding implication of climate change for water infrastructure is that the historical hydrological record — the basis for all infrastructure design standards — is no longer predictive. A 100-year flood event based on 20th century data may now occur every 20–30 years. A design-basis drought (the worst drought used to size reservoirs) may now be exceeded regularly. This means that water infrastructure designed to 20th century standards is systematically undersized for 21st century conditions. The AWWA’s "Beyond the Replacement Era" report (February 2026) explicitly identifies climate resilience as a new cost driver on top of aging asset replacement, PFAS compliance, and lead service line replacement — compounding the funding challenge.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: The Digital Water Utility  The Digital Water Utility

AI and IoT are transforming water system operations from reactive (respond to breaks and contamination after they occur) to predictive (identify likely failures and interventions before they happen). Xylem’s FLUID platform uses machine learning to predict water main breaks 1–3 years in advance based on pipe age, material, soil conditions, pressure history, and failure history. Digital twin technology (Bentley Systems, Xylem, Innovyze) creates real-time virtual models of entire water networks, enabling operators to simulate the impact of operational decisions before implementing them. Smart meters transmit hourly data that AI systems analyze for leak detection, demand pattern anomalies, and illegal connections. The data center water demand surge is also driving AI adoption: hyperscalers’ water consumption (2–5 liters per kWh for cooling) is creating sophisticated water management demand in data center facilities management.

Trend 2: Potable Water Reuse  Potable Water Reuse (Direct and Indirect)

Recycling treated wastewater to drinking water quality — once politically taboo ("toilet to tap") — is becoming essential in water-scarce regions. Orange County Water District’s Groundwater Replenishment System (189 million gallons/day, Southern California) is the world’s largest potable reuse facility. Denver Water, Colorado Springs, and Texas cities (Wichita Falls, Big Spring) have implemented direct or indirect potable reuse programs. Singapore’s NEWater (reclaimed water) provides 40% of the nation’s water needs. The regulatory pathway for direct potable reuse (DPR) — treating wastewater to drinking water standards and adding it directly to the distribution system without an environmental buffer — is being established state by state (California, Texas, Colorado leading). DPR can provide a drought-resilient local water supply for $0.80–1.50/m³, competitive with imported water or desalination in many regions.

Trend 3: Desalination Technology Advances  Desalination Technology Advances

Reverse osmosis membrane efficiency continues improving: energy consumption for seawater RO has fallen from 10–20 kWh/m³ (1970s) to 3–4 kWh/m³ in state-of-the-art plants today, approaching the thermodynamic minimum of 1 kWh/m³. Renewable-integrated desalination (solar or wind-powered RO) is reducing the carbon footprint and operating cost by 20–30% in sunny/windy locations. Small-scale containerized desalination units are enabling off-grid water supply for remote communities and disaster relief. ForwardWater (Toronto), Trevi Systems, and Porifera are developing forward osmosis and pressure-retarded osmosis technologies that could further reduce energy consumption. The global desalination market is growing at 9% CAGR driven by water scarcity and improving economics.

Trend 4: Water Rights Markets and Financial Innovation  Water Rights Markets and Financial Innovation

In water-scarce U.S. western states, water rights are becoming financial assets traded in emerging markets. The Colorado Water Exchange, Arizona Water Banking Authority, and California’s water transfer market are all examples of market-based water allocation mechanisms. The CME launched water futures contracts in December 2020 (California water spot price; NQH2O) — the first financial market for water in history. Institutional investors (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, agricultural investors) are buying water rights and agricultural water entitlements as inflation-protected, scarcity-driven assets. The Resnick Empire (Wonderful Company — pistachios, almonds, mandarin oranges) owns more water rights in California’s San Joaquin Valley than many small countries. Water rights acquisition by investment funds is politically contentious but financially compelling.

Trend 5: Emerging Contaminant Technology Wave  PFAS and Emerging Contaminant Technology

PFAS is the leading edge of a broader "emerging contaminant" challenge that includes microplastics, pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs), endocrine-disrupting compounds, and cyanotoxins from harmful algal blooms (HABs). The technology response is creating a new generation of advanced treatment companies: Aclarity (electrochemical PFAS destruction), 374Water (supercritical water oxidation), Gradiant (selective treatment of industrial water), and Organica Water (biological treatment with living root systems). The EPA’s expanding contaminant monitoring requirements (Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule, UCMR5 and UCMR6) are identifying new substances requiring treatment. Each new MCL creates a treatment market that didn’t exist before regulation.

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

Regulated Utility Investing: Regulated Utility Investing (Public Equities): Water utilities (AWK, WTRG, CWT, MSEX, SJW) trade as bond proxies — stable dividends, predictable rate base growth (7–10%/year), and low correlation to economic cycles. Key valuation metrics: EV/Rate Base (typically 1.3–1.7× for well-regulated utilities), P/E (25–35× for water vs. 15–20× for electric utilities reflecting water’s superior regulatory compact), and dividend yield (2–3%). Rate case analysis — understanding how state regulators set allowed returns on equity (typically 9–11% ROE for water) and how capital investment flows through the rate base into earnings — is the core technical competency.

Infrastructure PE: Infrastructure PE: The regulated water utility model — monopoly service territory, essential service, inflation-linked tariffs — is ideal for infrastructure PE. KKR, Brookfield, and Stonepeak have all established or expanded water platforms. Deal flow: acquisitions of investor-owned utilities from industrials divesting non-core assets, municipal system privatizations, and desalination project equity. The PFAS compliance mandate and lead service line replacement are creating capital deployment opportunities as utilities need partners with capital and operational expertise.

Water Technology VC: Water Technology VC and Growth Equity: Smart metering, digital twin, PFAS treatment technology, water recycling, and desalination innovation are active VC and growth equity categories. Burnt Island Ventures, XPV Water Partners, and Xylem Impact Fund are dedicated water technology VCs. Key metrics: cost per gallon treated/saved, regulatory approval status (EPA certification for drinking water), utility customer adoption, and scalability of capital requirements. Water technology businesses with SaaS-like economics (AMI analytics, water management platforms) attract traditional VC; hardware-intensive businesses (treatment systems) require more patient capital.

Investment Banking in Water: Investment Banking in Water Infrastructure: Regulated utility M&A (Lazard, Evercore, Barclays advise on water utility transactions); project finance for desalination plants (ING, Natixis, SMBC, MUFG active in Middle East and APAC water PPPs); municipal finance for water infrastructure bonds (Citigroup, Bank of America lead water revenue bonds). Key transaction types: rate base acquisition financing, WPA-backed desalination project finance, and PFAS settlement deployment advisory.

Key financial concepts: regulated rate base and allowed return on equity (ROE) mechanics; rate case process (filing, test year, discovery, settlement, commission decision); water purchase agreement (WPA) structure for desalination PPPs; non-revenue water economics (each 1% reduction in 20% NRW system saves millions in treatment and energy cost); PFAS remediation capital cost and compliance timeline analysis; the "cost of service" regulatory model vs. performance-based regulation; and the IIJA funding programs (DWSRF, CWSRF, $30B for drinking water, $56B for water/wastewater).

FUTURE OUTLOOK

Water infrastructure is in the early stages of the most significant investment cycle in its history. The compounding of aging system replacement, PFAS remediation, lead service line replacement, climate resilience upgrades, and digital transformation creates a 25-year capital deployment opportunity that dwarfs anything the sector has previously experienced. The fundamental challenge is not the availability of investment opportunity but the structure of financing: regulated utilities cannot unilaterally raise rates fast enough to fund all needs, federal programs are insufficient, and private capital requires appropriate return structures to justify long-duration investment.

Base Case: Base Case: U.S. water utility consolidation continues (20–30 large investor-owned utilities by 2035 vs. 50,000+ fragmented utilities today); PFAS compliance drives $80B+ in treatment infrastructure by 2031; desalination grows to 2–3% of global freshwater supply (from 1% today); digital water technology (smart metering, AI-driven ops) becomes standard across utilities serving 100,000+ connections; water rates rise 50–100% in nominal terms over 20 years as full cost recovery becomes necessary.

Bull Case: Bull Case: Federal infrastructure investment increases significantly; potable water reuse achieves mainstream regulatory approval in 30+ states by 2030; solar-powered desalination reaches $0.30–0.40/m³ (competitive with groundwater in many markets); water technology sector becomes the fastest-growing infrastructure investment category; CME water futures market matures to provide hedging for water-dependent industries.

Bear Case: Bear Case: Affordability crisis — PFAS compliance costs push water bills beyond low-income affordability thresholds, triggering political backlash and rate case denials that delay infrastructure investment; Colorado River compact collapse forces radical rationing in Southwest; PFAS litigation creates utility insolvencies; climate-driven flooding destroys investment in water infrastructure faster than it can be rebuilt; private investment in water triggers public ownership backlash.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"Water is the most essential infrastructure there is — you can live 3 weeks without food, 3 days without water. Yet the ASCE gives U.S. drinking water a C+ and wastewater a D+ in 2025. The cumulative funding gap is $2.1 to $2.4 trillion just for drinking water over the next 25 years. That’s why American Water Works just merged with Essential Utilities to create a $63B combined utility — you need scale to execute PFAS remediation programs, lead service line replacement, and climate resilience upgrades simultaneously when your regulatory compact only allows 9–11% return on equity."

"PFAS is the defining water challenge of the decade. 45%+ of U.S. tap water contains PFAS at detectable levels. The EPA set limits at 4 parts per trillion in 2024 — so stringent that virtually every utility needs to upgrade treatment systems. The total cost is $80B+. 3M’s $10.3B settlement proceeds started flowing to utilities in 2025. The technology winners are granular activated carbon systems, ion exchange, and the next-generation PFAS destruction technologies that Aclarity and 374Water are commercializing. It’s the largest new water treatment investment cycle in 30 years."

"Desalination is no longer a technology of last resort — it’s a national security infrastructure asset. Israel gets 80% of its drinking water from desalination. Saudi Arabia and the UAE produce virtually all theirs that way. When the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed in early 2026, Gulf nations faced acute water security stress because their desalination plants run on energy that suddenly became expensive and unreliable. That’s why renewable-integrated desalination — solar or wind-powered reverse osmosis — is growing so fast. Water security and energy security are the same problem in water-scarce nations."

"The most underappreciated water investment story in the U.S. is the Colorado River. The river’s legal allocations were set in 1922 during the wettest decade in a millennium. The 1922 Compact allocates 17.5 million acre-feet per year; the river’s actual average flow since 2000 is 12–13 million acre-feet. We’ve been spending reserves built up over centuries for 25 years. Lake Mead and Lake Powell at historic lows. 40 million people in Arizona, Nevada, California, Utah, Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico — plus millions of acres of agriculture — depend on that river. Water rights in Arizona are trading at prices that reflect their true scarcity, and that market is just beginning to develop."
