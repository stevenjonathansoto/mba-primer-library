HISTORY & ORIGINS: FROM SPINDLETOP TO SHALE

The history of oil is the history of modern industrial civilization. No commodity has shaped geopolitics, economic development, warfare, and technology more profoundly in the past 125 years. Understanding the historical arc — from the first commercial wells to the shale revolution to the current managed-decline era — is essential context for any oil investing or strategy conversation.

The Birth of the Modern Oil Industry (1859–1950)

Edwin Drake's well in Titusville, Pennsylvania (1859) is the conventional starting point of the modern oil industry, though oil had been extracted from seeps in Baku (Azerbaijan), Borneo, and Peru for decades prior. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil (founded 1870) created the template for the integrated oil company: controlling refining (80%+ of U.S. refining capacity by 1880), pipelines, and marketing. The Supreme Court's antitrust breakup of Standard Oil (1911) created the predecessor companies of today's supermajors: Esso (now ExxonMobil), Mobil (Exxon-Mobil), Socony (Mobil), Chevron (Standard Oil of California), and Amoco (BP). The Spindletop discovery in Texas (1901) opened the Gulf Coast as the dominant U.S. production region; the East Texas field (1930) created the modern oil surplus problem. The Seven Sisters — Esso, Royal Dutch Shell, BP, Gulf Oil, Texaco, Mobil, and Chevron — carved up global oil production in the 1928 Achnacarry Agreement, effectively controlling world oil supply for 30 years.

The OPEC Era and the First Oil Shocks (1960–1985)

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) was founded in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela — originally as a negotiating bloc against the Seven Sisters' ability to unilaterally set posted prices. The real power shift came in 1973: in response to U.S. support for Israel in the Yom Kippur War, Arab OPEC members imposed an oil embargo. Oil prices quadrupled from $3/barrel to $12/barrel in six months. The 1973 shock reshaped the global economy — triggering stagflation, accelerating fuel efficiency standards, and demonstrating that a cartel of producing nations could override Western oil companies' pricing power.

The 1979 Iranian Revolution and subsequent Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988) created the second oil shock, pushing prices to $35/barrel (equivalent to $130+ in 2024 dollars). The shocks triggered massive investment in non-OPEC production (North Sea, Alaska North Slope, Mexico offshore) that flooded the market by 1986, crashing prices to $10/barrel in the "oil price collapse." Saudi Arabia's decision to defend market share over price (opening the taps in 1985) remains the template for the 2014–2016 and 2020 price wars.

The Modern Era: Globalization, the Gulf War, and Pre-Shale Supply (1990–2007)

The Gulf War (1990–1991) validated the U.S. strategic interest in Persian Gulf oil security and established the principle that U.S. military power would be deployed to protect Gulf oil flows — a doctrine that defined U.S. foreign policy for 30 years. The 1990s were characterized by low oil prices ($15–20/barrel), major deepwater discoveries (Gulf of Mexico, Angola, Brazil), and the consolidation wave that created the modern supermajors: Exxon-Mobil ($81B, 1999), BP-Amoco-ARCO ($110B, 1998–2000), Chevron-Texaco ($45B, 2001), and ConocoPhillips. The 2003 Iraq War triggered the first leg of the 2003–2008 supercycle: demand growth from China's industrial boom combined with constrained supply growth to push prices from $25/barrel to $147/barrel by July 2008.

The Shale Revolution: America's Energy Transformation (2008–2019)

The shale revolution — the commercial application of horizontal drilling combined with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing to unlock oil and gas from low-permeability shale formations — is the most significant structural change in global oil markets since OPEC's 1973 embargo. The technology was not new: hydraulic fracturing had been used since the 1940s, and George Mitchell of Mitchell Energy had been experimenting with shale completions since the 1990s. What changed in 2008–2010 was the combination of $100/barrel oil prices (making the economics work), improved horizontal drilling technology, and the application of fracturing to tight oil formations (the Bakken in North Dakota, the Eagle Ford in Texas, and most importantly the Permian Basin in West Texas and New Mexico).

The results were historically unprecedented. U.S. oil production, which had been in decline since 1970 (from 9.6 mb/d peak to 5 mb/d trough in 2008), reversed course and rose from 5 mb/d in 2008 to 13 mb/d by 2019 — making the U.S. the world's largest oil producer, surpassing Saudi Arabia and Russia. This 8 mb/d increase in U.S. production in 11 years is the largest sustained increase in oil production in the history of the industry. The consequences were global: OPEC lost its role as swing producer; the global oil price equilibrium was fundamentally reset; and the U.S. ended nearly 50 years of import dependence, becoming a net oil exporter in 2019 for the first time since 1949.

OPEC's response was dramatic. In November 2014, Saudi Arabia — seeing U.S. shale taking market share — abandoned its price-defense strategy and opened production to flood the market. Oil prices fell from $107/barrel in June 2014 to $27/barrel in January 2016. The strategy aimed to eliminate high-cost shale producers who had leveraged up at $100/barrel prices. The result was partial: hundreds of small shale companies went bankrupt, the U.S. rig count fell 80%, but the largest and most efficient shale operators survived, cut costs dramatically, and emerged as the most efficient oil producers in the world. The attempted Saudi-OPEC strategy to kill shale failed — shale costs fell from $60–70/barrel breakeven to $35–45/barrel, making it resilient at any price that keeps the industry viable long-term.

OPEC+ and Managed Markets (2016–Present)

In December 2016, OPEC and 10 non-OPEC producers led by Russia formed OPEC+ — the most significant expansion of the cartel's influence since its founding. Russia joined not because it wanted to coordinate with OPEC ideologically but because $27/barrel oil was destroying Russian government revenues. The OPEC+ framework gave Saudi Arabia and Russia joint control over ≥ 25% of global oil supply — enough to meaningfully influence prices when deployed carefully. The COVID-19 oil crash (April 2020: WTI futures briefly went negative, -$37/barrel, as storage filled) was the most extreme oil market event in history and forced unprecedented OPEC+ coordination (9.7 mb/d of cuts). The recovery (2021–2022) brought oil back to $100+, supermajors generated record profits, and OPEC+ began the gradual unwind of its production cuts that continued through 2025.

THE OIL MARKET STRUCTURE

Upstream, Midstream, Downstream: The Value Chain

The oil industry's value chain has three distinct segments with very different risk profiles, capital structures, and valuation frameworks.

Segment

Economics, Risk & Key Players

Upstream (Exploration & Production)

Finding, developing, and producing crude oil. The highest-risk, highest-reward segment. Key metrics: reserve life (ratio of reserves to annual production), finding and development cost (F&D cost, $/barrel), production cost per barrel, netback (price minus cost of getting to market), and reserve replacement ratio. IOCs (ExxonMobil, Shell, Chevron, BP, TotalEnergies) and NOCs dominate large-scale production; independents (Pioneer, Devon, EOG, Diamondback) are major U.S. shale players. Valuation: discounted cash flow of reserve base (PV10: present value at 10% discount of proven reserves), EV/EBITDA (typically 4–8× for large integrated players, 3–6× for pure upstream), and NAV (net asset value per share). Reserve replacement is the existential metric: a company must find and develop as much oil each year as it produces, or it is liquidating itself.

Midstream (Transport & Storage)

Pipelines, terminals, storage, and gathering systems that move crude oil from wellhead to refinery. Primarily a fee-based business: midstream companies charge a tolling fee per barrel regardless of oil price. This creates an "energy infrastructure" business model with stable, contracted cash flows independent of commodity price — closer to regulated utility than oil company. Publicly traded via Master Limited Partnerships (MLPs: Enterprise Products Partners, Magellan Midstream, Williams Companies, Kinder Morgan) or C-corporations. EBITDA multiples 8–12× for core pipeline systems; distribution yields 5–8%. The Permian Basin pipeline buildout (2018–2023) was $40B+ in new pipeline infrastructure serving the shale production surge.

Downstream (Refining & Marketing)

Converting crude oil into refined products (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, fuel oil, petrochemicals) and distributing to end users. Refining economics are driven by the "crack spread" — the price differential between crude input and refined product output. Complex refineries ("crackers") can process heavy, sour crude into high-value products; simple refineries are more limited. The Hormuz crisis dramatically tightened refining margins as middle distillate cracks hit all-time highs in Q1 2026. The U.S. Gulf Coast is the world's most competitive refining region: Marathon Petroleum, Valero, and Phillips 66 are the largest U.S. independent refiners. Downstream is intentionally lighter in this primer — the primary complexity is in upstream and OPEC dynamics.

OPEC+: HOW THE CARTEL WORKS

OPEC+ is the most consequential economic cartel in the world — 13 OPEC members plus 10 non-OPEC allies, collectively controlling roughly 50% of global oil production and 70% of proven reserves. Understanding how it works, why it sometimes fails, and what drives Saudi Arabia's decisions is essential for any oil market conversation.

The Cartel Mechanics

OPEC+ sets production targets for each member country at ministerial meetings (typically twice annually, with emergency meetings as needed). Each country receives a "quota" — a maximum production level. The cartel's power derives from two structural advantages: Saudi Arabia has 2–3 mb/d of spare capacity (the ability to increase production within 90 days and sustain it), which it can deploy as a credible threat against non-compliant members or external competitors; and the combined OPEC+ share of ~50% of global supply is large enough that 1–2 mb/d of coordinated cuts or additions can meaningfully move the global price. The weakness: quota compliance varies dramatically by member. Iraq, Kazakhstan, Russia, and the UAE have chronically produced above their quotas. Saudi Arabia has effectively subsidized others' non-compliance by making deeper-than-required own cuts.

The Saudi Arabia Pivot:  Market Share vs. Price Defense

Saudi Arabia has historically had two OPEC strategies available: price defense (cut production to hold prices up, accepting market share loss to higher-cost producers) or market share defense (open production to drive prices below the marginal cost of high-cost producers, accepting lower near-term revenue to eliminate competition). The 2014–2016 episode was market share defense against shale; it failed because shale costs fell faster than anticipated. From 2016–2022, Saudi Arabia returned to price defense (OPEC+ cuts). From mid-2025, Saudi Arabia began again using production increases to punish non-compliant members — a partial market share signal. The Hormuz crisis in early 2026 suspended this dynamic entirely, as the physical closure of the strait created the tightest oil market in history regardless of OPEC+ policy.

OPEC+ Compliance and Cheating Dynamics

Every OPEC member faces the same game-theoretic tension: if all members comply with cuts, prices rise and everyone benefits; but each individual member can produce more than its quota and capture an even larger benefit if others comply. This classic prisoner's dilemma creates chronic compliance problems. Secondary sources (Platts, Argus, JODI, IEA) report actual production independently of member self-reporting, creating some accountability. Saudi Arabia has periodically made "compensation cuts" above its quota to offset others' overproduction — effectively subsidizing non-compliant members' free-riding, a structural weakness of any cartel. The OPEC+ Monitoring Committee (JMMC) reviews compliance data monthly; countries with excess production are supposed to "compensate" with future below-quota production. In practice, compensation rarely materializes in full.

THE U.S. SHALE REVOLUTION: PERMIAN DOMINANCE

The shale revolution permanently restructured global oil markets by making the U.S. the world's swing producer. Understanding shale economics, the Permian Basin specifically, and how major M&A has consolidated the industry is essential for any upstream oil conversation.

How Horizontal Drilling and Hydraulic Fracturing Work  How Shale Works

Conventional oil exists in porous reservoir rock (sandstone, carbonate) where oil flows freely to the wellbore. Shale oil is trapped in the source rock itself — the dense, low-permeability shale where the oil was originally generated. Extracting it requires two innovations: (1) Horizontal drilling: the wellbore is drilled vertically to the target shale formation (typically 1–3 miles deep), then turned horizontal within the shale layer for 1–2 miles, maximizing contact with the oil-bearing rock. (2) Hydraulic fracturing ("fracking"): water, sand, and chemicals are pumped at high pressure into the horizontal wellbore, creating a network of fractures in the rock. Sand ("proppant") holds the fractures open, allowing oil to flow through the fracture network to the wellbore. A modern Permian Basin well costs $6–8M to drill and complete, produces 500–800 barrels/day initially, and has a steep decline curve (70% in year 1, 50% year 2), requiring continuous drilling to maintain production. Unlike conventional fields that produce for decades with minimal investment, shale is a "treadmill" requiring constant capital investment.

The Permian Basin:  The Engine of U.S. Production

The Permian Basin (West Texas and southeastern New Mexico) is the world's most prolific oil-producing region, producing 6.3 mb/d as of 2025 — more than any OPEC member except Saudi Arabia. The Permian is unique among shale plays: it has multiple stacked pay zones (the Wolfcamp, Spraberry, Bone Spring, and other formations), meaning a single surface location can access multiple distinct oil-bearing layers via separate horizontal wellbores. This stacking dramatically improves capital efficiency relative to single-zone plays like the Bakken or Eagle Ford. Core Permian acreage in the Midland and Delaware sub-basins has breakeven costs of $35–45/barrel — competitive with the cheapest conventional production in the world outside of the Persian Gulf. The ExxonMobil-Pioneer acquisition ($60B, 2024) created the dominant Permian operator with 1.3 million net acres and 16 billion BOE of resource. Chevron-Hess ($53B, 2024) combined Permian and deepwater Guyana assets. Diamondback Energy-Endeavor ($26B, 2024) created the largest pure-play Permian independent.

THE STRAIT OF HORMUZ: THE DEFINING CRISIS OF 2026

The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz following U.S.-Israeli military strikes on Iran (February 28, 2026) is the largest oil supply disruption in the history of the global oil market, as confirmed by IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol. Understanding this event — its geography, its mechanics, and its investment implications — is the single most important current affairs test for any energy recruiting conversation in 2026.

The Geography

The Strait of Hormuz is a 21-mile-wide chokepoint at the mouth of the Persian Gulf connecting to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Through this strait passes ~20 mb/d of crude oil, condensate, and oil products (20% of global oil consumption and 35% of seaborne crude trade) plus roughly 20% of global LNG (from Qatar's Ras Laffan terminal). There is no viable alternative routing for Persian Gulf oil: the Trans-Arabian Pipeline (Tapline) and East-West Pipeline (Saudi Arabia to Red Sea) have combined capacity of only 5–6 mb/d, far below the Hormuz throughput. Iran’s coast runs along the northern shore of the Strait, giving Tehran unique geographic leverage.

The 2026 Crisis Timeline

U.S. and Israeli forces struck Iranian nuclear facilities and military infrastructure on February 28, 2026. Iran responded by effectively closing the Strait: IRGC naval forces declared it closed on March 2; tanker traffic dropped precipitously as insurance became unwritable and major shipping lines (Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd) suspended transits. Over 150 tankers anchored outside the strait rather than risk attack. 10.5 mb/d of OPEC+ crude production was shut-in in March as Gulf producers lost their primary export route. The IEA reported global supply fell 10.1 mb/d to 97 mb/d — a decline comparable to the COVID crash but driven by geopolitics rather than demand destruction. Brent crude averaged $117/barrel in April 2026 — a $46/barrel increase from February and the highest monthly price since 2008.

The Policy Response and Market Outlook

The IEA took the unprecedented step of releasing 400 million barrels from member country strategic petroleum reserves — the largest coordinated SPR release in history. A temporary ceasefire was announced in early April 2026, allowing some shipping traffic to resume; oil prices eased somewhat but remained elevated amid uncertainty about the resolution timeline. EIA (May 2026) assumes the strait remains effectively closed until late May, with traffic beginning to normalize in June. EIA projects Brent at $106/barrel in Q2 2026, declining to $89/barrel by Q4 2026 as Middle East production resumes, and $79/barrel in 2027 as the supply shock fully reverses. The crisis has permanently elevated awareness of Persian Gulf concentration risk and accelerated conversations about SPR adequacy, pipeline alternative development, and the strategic logic of U.S. domestic oil production.

OIL PRICE MECHANICS: WHAT MOVES THE PRICE

Understanding what drives oil prices is among the most tested topics in energy finance interviews. The answer is not simple: oil price is determined by the intersection of global supply and demand, OPEC+ production decisions, macroeconomic conditions, geopolitical risk premia, financial market positioning, and the U.S. dollar exchange rate.

Price Driver

How It Works & Magnitude of Impact

OPEC+ Production Decisions

The single most important fundamental driver. 1 mb/d of sustained supply change typically moves prices $5–10/barrel over 3–6 months as inventory levels adjust. OPEC+ spare capacity (Saudi Arabia holds 2–3 mb/d of swingable capacity) provides a ceiling and floor: Saudi can lower prices by increasing production, or support prices by cutting. The 2026 Hormuz shock removed 10.5 mb/d — unprecedented and immediately pushed prices to $117/barrel.

Global Demand Growth (China + India + Emerging Markets)

China alone accounts for ~14 mb/d of demand (~15% of global consumption); India ~5 mb/d and growing at 200–300 kb/d annually; combined emerging market demand growth of 1–1.5 mb/d per year provides the structural demand floor that keeps the market tight. Demand slowdowns (GFC 2008, COVID 2020, tariff-driven slowdown 2025) create the sharpest price crashes.

U.S. Shale Supply Response

Shale's short-cycle nature (6–9 months from drilling decision to first production) makes U.S. production a near-term price moderator. When prices rise above $70/barrel, U.S. rig count rises and production grows; below $50/barrel, rig count falls and production declines. This creates a price band where U.S. shale effectively acts as OPEC's shadow capacity, moderating both upside and downside price moves.

Geopolitical Risk Premium

Oil markets embed a risk premium for potential supply disruptions: Iran nuclear tensions add $3–5/barrel; active conflicts (Libya civil war, Sudan, Yemen/Red Sea) add $2–5/barrel. Full supply disruptions (Iraq invasion 2003, Libya 2011, Hormuz 2026) add $20–50+/barrel. Risk premium evaporates quickly when threats recede, creating sharp price corrections.

Inventory Levels (IEA/EIA weekly data)

The OECD commercial oil inventory level (total barrels in tanks, pipelines, and tankers) is the most watched weekly data point. When inventories are "above 5-year average," prices are typically suppressed (excess supply visible). "Below 5-year average" is bullish. The EIA's weekly U.S. crude inventory release (Wednesday at 10:30 ET) moves oil markets immediately.

U.S. Dollar Strength

Oil is priced in U.S. dollars globally. A stronger dollar makes oil more expensive in local currency terms, reducing demand from non-dollar countries. A weaker dollar has the opposite effect. The dollar-oil inverse correlation is roughly -0.7 over rolling 12-month periods. Fed policy decisions that move the dollar therefore indirectly move oil prices.

Futures Market Positioning (COT Report)

Managed money positioning (hedge funds, CTAs) amplifies price moves. When speculators are massively long crude futures, a demand shock or OPEC+ surprise can trigger forced liquidation cascades, exaggerating the price decline beyond fundamentals. The CFTC Commitment of Traders (COT) report, published weekly, shows speculative positioning and is a leading indicator of potential price reversals.

ENERGY TRANSITION PRESSURE & STRANDED ASSET RISK

The existential question for the oil industry is whether the energy transition will create "stranded assets" — oil reserves that cannot be produced before demand falls to a level that makes them uneconomic. The answer depends entirely on which demand scenario you believe.

The Peak Demand Debate

There is perhaps no more consequential analytical disagreement in energy markets than when global oil demand will peak and at what level it will decline. The IEA's Stated Policies Scenario (STEPS) projects peak demand around 2030 at ~106 mb/d; its Net Zero Emissions scenario suggests demand has already peaked (2023 was the all-time high). BP's Energy Outlook clusters peak demand in 2025–2030 depending on the scenario. ExxonMobil's Global Outlook projects oil demand growing through 2040 and remaining above 100 mb/d at 2050. The divergence is not primarily about different economic modeling — it reflects fundamentally different assumptions about EV adoption speed, efficiency improvement, emerging market demand growth, and the pace of policy implementation. Each scenario has major investment implications: in a 2030 peak demand world, oil projects with 10+ year payback periods are stranded; in a 2040 demand-growth world, underinvestment today creates the next price spike.

Scope 3 Emissions and Divestment Pressure  Scope 3 Emissions and Institutional Divestment

The most acute ESG pressure on oil companies comes from Scope 3 emissions — the emissions generated when customers burn the oil the company sells. For an oil producer, Scope 3 is typically 80–85% of its total emissions footprint. The question of whether an oil company is responsible for Scope 3 emissions is unresolved: oil companies argue they are selling a legal product and cannot control how customers use it; NGOs and some investors argue that Scope 3 is the central climate risk in oil company valuations. Institutional divestment from fossil fuels has grown significantly: the GFANZ (Glasgow Financial Alliance for Net Zero) committed $130T in financial assets to net-zero by 2050, which requires portfolio companies to have credible transition plans. But the largest sovereign wealth funds (Norway's NBIM retains oil holdings despite the ethical paradox of its origins), pension funds, and asset managers remain the primary oil shareholders — most have adopted engagement rather than divestment as their primary approach.

The Counter-Argument: Underinvestment Risk

The IEA, despite being the primary institutional voice for energy transition, has also warned repeatedly that underinvestment in conventional oil supply creates the conditions for the next price spike. Oil fields decline naturally at 4–6% per year without new investment. If global demand is 100 mb/d in 2030 and existing fields decline at 5% per year, the industry needs to find and develop 30+ mb/d of new supply over the next 5 years just to prevent a supply crisis. This replacement investment requirement is happening even in a world of peak demand. The Hormuz crisis of 2026 validated this concern: when supply is disrupted even temporarily, prices spike violently because there is insufficient spare capacity and alternative routing to absorb the shock.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

The oil market faces the most uncertain decade in its history. Demand trajectory depends on EV adoption speed, emerging market growth, and efficiency improvements that are genuinely uncertain. Supply trajectory depends on OPEC+ discipline, shale efficiency, and the pace of conventional field development. Geopolitical risk (Hormuz, Russia, Venezuela, Libya) can override any fundamental analysis in the short term. The investment implication: companies with the lowest-cost, shortest-payback assets are the most resilient across scenarios; those dependent on long-cycle, high-cost projects face stranded asset risk in lower-demand scenarios.

Base Case: Base Case: Global oil demand peaks at ~106–108 mb/d around 2028–2030, then declines slowly (1–2% per year); Brent crude averages $70–80/barrel through 2030 as OPEC+ manages supply; U.S. shale plateaus at ~13 mb/d; Permian continues to be the dominant growth basin; Guyana and Brazil offshore grow significantly; Hormuz tensions gradually resolve through 2026–2027 with prices normalizing to $79–89/barrel range.

Bull Case: Bull Case: Emerging market demand growth exceeds projections; EV adoption slower than forecast; Middle East instability keeps geopolitical risk premium elevated; OPEC+ discipline holds; underinvestment in conventional supply creates supply deficit by 2028; Brent $90–110/barrel sustained.

Bear Case: Bear Case: EV adoption accelerates dramatically (China EV penetration 60%+ by 2030); global economic slowdown reduces demand; OPEC+ discipline breaks as members prioritize market share; U.S. shale efficiency continues improving; oil demand peaks below 105 mb/d by 2027; Brent sustained at $50–65/barrel forcing write-downs of high-cost assets (oil sands, deep ultradeep offshore).

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"The Hormuz crisis is the most consequential oil supply disruption in history — 10.5 mb/d shut-in in a single month, larger than the 1973 Arab embargo. What makes it structurally different from prior disruptions is that there is no bypass route: the Tapline and East-West Pipeline together can handle maybe 5 mb/d, and they’re not fully operational. When the strait closes, Persian Gulf oil has nowhere to go. That’s why prices hit $117/barrel in April 2026 and why the IEA released 400 million barrels from strategic reserves. The geographic lesson: 20% of global oil supply flows through a 21-mile-wide chokepoint. That concentration has no equivalent anywhere in global energy."

"The shale revolution is the most important structural change in oil markets since OPEC’s 1973 embargo — but in the opposite direction. OPEC’s 1973 move demonstrated that a cartel of producers could override Western companies’ pricing power. The shale revolution demonstrated that private-sector technology and capital could create more swing production capacity than OPEC controls. The U.S. went from 5 mb/d in 2008 to 13.4 mb/d in 2025. That 8.4 mb/d increase is the largest sustained production ramp in oil industry history. And the Permian Basin alone produces more than every OPEC member except Saudi Arabia."

"The five supermajors have five completely different energy transition strategies, and it’s not clear which one is right. ExxonMobil says demand grows through 2040 — buy more oil assets. BP said in 2020 it would cut production 40% and pivot to renewables — then reversed most of it by 2024 because the financial returns were terrible. TotalEnergies is trying to genuinely be a multi-energy company: big in oil AND big in solar AND big in wind. Shell has restructured toward LNG and reduced its renewable ambition. The market has rewarded the most oil-focused strategies (ExxonMobil, Chevron) and punished the most aggressive energy transition strategies (BP, to some extent Shell). That outcome has shaped the entire industry’s posture."

"OPEC’s fundamental problem is the prisoner’s dilemma. Every member benefits if all comply with cuts. But each individual member can produce above quota and capture even more benefit if others comply. Saudi Arabia has essentially been subsidizing others’ non-compliance by cutting deeper than required. Iraq chronically overproduces. Kazakhstan says it’s committed but then doesn’t compensate. Saudi Arabia’s periodic decision to open production — 2014, 2020, 2025 — is not irrational; it’s a credible punishment strategy to make cheating more costly. The question is always whether the threat is credible and whether Saudi’s budget can withstand low prices long enough to discipline the cheaters."
