INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Private equity's involvement in energy and infrastructure is not a single story — it is three overlapping stories happening simultaneously across the same asset class. Infrastructure PE firms (Brookfield, Macquarie, KKR Infrastructure) treat energy assets like regulated utilities: long-duration, contracted cash flows, inflation-linked returns. Energy-focused buyout PE firms (EQT, Stonepeak, Energy Capital Partners) apply LBO mechanics to energy companies and operating platforms, targeting operational value creation and multiple expansion. And a third category — generalist mega-funds (Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle) — has moved aggressively into energy since 2020, deploying credit, equity, and infrastructure capital across the full energy stack simultaneously. For MBA candidates, understanding how PE participates in energy means understanding all three strategies, how deal structures differ fundamentally from traditional buyouts, and why the IRA and OBBBA have reshuffled which energy assets are attracting capital and which are being avoided.

LBO MECHANICS: THE FOUNDATION (AND WHY ENERGY IS DIFFERENT)

Before understanding how PE participates in energy and infrastructure specifically, you need to understand the core private equity deal mechanics — and then understand how energy assets modify every element of that framework. This section assumes limited prior PE knowledge and builds from first principles.

The Traditional LBO: How PE Works

A leveraged buyout (LBO) is the foundational PE transaction structure. A PE firm acquires a company using a combination of equity capital (from its fund, typically 30–40% of the purchase price) and debt (60–70% of the purchase price, borrowed against the acquired company's assets and cash flows). The logic: using debt amplifies equity returns. If you buy a company for $1B using $600M of debt and $400M of equity, and sell it for $1.5B five years later, you've made $500M on a $400M equity investment — a 25% IRR — even though the enterprise value only increased by 50%.

The three levers of LBO value creation are: (1) Revenue growth — organic growth, pricing power, or market expansion that increases EBITDA over the hold period. (2) Margin improvement — cost reduction, operational efficiency, or mix shift that improves EBITDA margins. (3) Multiple expansion — buying at a lower EV/EBITDA multiple than you sell at, either through market re-rating, strategic positioning, or exit timing. The fourth lever, debt paydown — using the company's free cash flow to pay down acquisition debt during the hold period — mechanically increases equity value as leverage falls.

The PE fund structure: a fund raises committed capital from LPs (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, insurance companies) over a 12–18 month fundraise. Capital is drawn down as investments are made. The standard fund life is 10 years (5 years of investment + 5 years of harvesting), extendable with LP consent. The GP earns a management fee (typically 1.5–2% of committed capital) and carried interest (20% of profits above the 8% preferred return hurdle). The LP commitment is illiquid for the fund life — the primary constraint that shapes everything else about how PE funds operate.

How Energy Assets Modify Every Element of the LBO Framework

Energy and infrastructure assets force PE investors to rethink almost every standard LBO assumption. Here is a systematic comparison:

LBO Element

How It Changes in Energy & Infrastructure

Target Company Type

Traditional LBO: a corporation with EBITDA, employees, and operating leverage. Energy PE: may be a single asset (a power plant, a pipeline, an LNG terminal), a portfolio of projects, or an operating company that owns and manages physical energy infrastructure. Single-asset acquisitions require project finance structures rather than corporate LBO structures.

Debt Structure

Traditional LBO: corporate leveraged loans, high-yield bonds, revolving credit facility, typically 4–6× Net Debt/EBITDA. Energy PE: varies dramatically by asset type. Contracted or regulated assets support higher leverage (5–7×) secured by project cash flows (non-recourse project finance). Merchant assets (no long-term contracts) support lower leverage (3–4×). Tax equity sits outside the debt structure entirely.

Revenue Model

Traditional LBO: product or service sales with customer concentration risk and competitive dynamics. Energy PE: contracted (PPA, tolling agreement, regulated tariff) or merchant (spot market prices). Contracted revenue enables investment-grade project debt. Merchant revenue creates commodity price risk that drives lower leverage and higher equity return targets.

Tax Equity Layer

Traditional LBO: no equivalent. Energy PE: for renewable energy, carbon capture, storage, and nuclear projects, federal tax credits (ITC, PTC, 45Q, 45U) generate significant value — but only for entities with sufficient U.S. tax liability. Tax equity investors (banks, insurance companies with large tax bills) purchase the right to receive the credits. This creates a third capital layer between senior debt and sponsor equity that has no analog in traditional buyouts.

Credit Transfer (Post-IRA)

Traditional LBO: no equivalent. Energy PE: since the IRA (2022), ITC/PTC can be sold directly to third parties (transferred) without requiring the buyer to be involved in the project. PE firms now purchase transferred credits at a discount (typically $0.92–$0.97 per $1.00 of credits) as a distinct yield investment. The $30B+ annual transfer market is a new PE-adjacent asset class.

Hold Period

Traditional LBO: 3–7 years (average ~5). Energy PE: varies by strategy. Development-stage assets: shorter hold (3–5 years through construction and initial operation, then exit to infrastructure fund). Operating infrastructure: longer hold (7–15 years). Open-end infrastructure funds (Brookfield, Macquarie) have no fixed exit requirement, enabling permanent capital model.

Value Creation Levers

Traditional LBO: revenue growth, margin expansion, multiple expansion, debt paydown. Energy PE: contracted revenue optimization (renegotiating PPAs), operational efficiency (improved capacity factors, lower O&M), capital recycling (refinancing at better rates, adding debt as asset de-risks), development optionality (adding capacity to existing sites), and exit to a larger infrastructure buyer at a lower cost of capital (yield compression).

Exit Options

Traditional LBO: strategic buyer M&A, IPO, secondary buyout, dividend recapitalization. Energy PE: strategic buyer (utility, energy major, hyperscaler buying power assets), secondary sale to another PE firm, IPO of an infrastructure company or REIT/YieldCo, project bond refinancing (taking out equity), or sale to an open-end infrastructure fund seeking stable yield.

THE THREE PE STRATEGIES IN ENERGY & INFRASTRUCTURE

PE's involvement in energy is not monolithic. Three distinct strategies operate in the same asset class with different return targets, LP bases, hold periods, and value creation approaches. Understanding the differences — and which firms exemplify each — is essential for any energy PE recruiting conversation.

Strategy 1: Infrastructure PE — The Contracted Cash Flow Model

Infrastructure PE firms treat energy assets as infrastructure: long-duration, essential service, contracted or regulated revenue, inflation-linked cash flows, and institutionally managed for the long term. They are not trying to create value through operational transformation or strategic repositioning. They are trying to acquire assets at a price that delivers a target yield over a 10–20+ year hold, finance them efficiently, and pass through inflation-linked cash flows to LPs. This is the lowest-risk, lowest-return PE strategy in energy — but the largest by AUM.

The target return is unlevered IRR of 9–12% (core-plus to value-add infrastructure) or levered equity IRR of 12–18% for more complex acquisitions. The LP base is pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and insurance companies who need long-duration, inflation-protected income. The defining transaction: buying a 500 MW contracted solar portfolio from a developer for $800M, placing $480M of non-recourse project finance against it, and earning a stable yield over the 15-year PPA tenor, with an exit to a yield-oriented buyer.

Key infrastructure PE firms in energy: Brookfield Asset Management (~$104B infrastructure AUM, NYSE: BN) — the global leader, with renewable power and transition as its largest energy strategy; KKR Infrastructure ($81.6B raised 2020–2024) — energy transition, digital infrastructure, pipelines; Global Infrastructure Partners (now part of BlackRock) — energy, transport, digital; Macquarie Asset Management (~$700B total); Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners (CIP) — offshore wind specialist, $30B+; EQT Infrastructure — European focus expanding globally.

Strategy 2: Energy-Focused Buyout PE — The Operating Platform Model

Energy-focused buyout PE firms apply traditional PE value creation mechanics to energy operating companies and platforms. The target is not a single contracted asset but a company — a power generation platform, a midstream operator, a data center developer, or an energy services business — where operational improvement, strategic repositioning, and M&A can drive EBITDA growth over a 5–7 year hold. Return targets are higher than infrastructure PE (levered equity IRR of 18–25%+) and risk tolerance is correspondingly higher.

The defining transaction type: buying a power generation company (a fleet of gas-fired plants, a regional renewable developer, a data center power platform) at a multiple that reflects current earnings, executing a value creation plan (operational improvement, capacity additions, geographic expansion, margin improvement), and exiting at a higher multiple to a strategic buyer or larger infrastructure fund. This is where traditional LBO skills translate most directly into energy — with the additional complexity of regulated and contracted revenue structures.

Key energy-focused buyout PE firms: Stonepeak ($73B AUM, New York) — focuses on defensive, hard-asset infrastructure with a PE-style operational improvement lens; Blackstone Infrastructure ($32.5B infrastructure raised) — Blackstone Real Estate and credit platforms converge in energy; Energy Capital Partners (New Jersey, $30B+ energy AUM) — power generation, transmission, renewables; Ares Infrastructure (part of Ares Management's $40B+ infrastructure platform); ArcLight Capital Partners — midstream, power, energy services.

Strategy 3: Generalist Mega-Fund Energy Strategies — The Multi-Asset Approach

The largest alternative asset managers — Blackstone, Apollo, KKR (as a combined platform), Carlyle — have moved aggressively into energy using multiple capital pools simultaneously. They invest equity from their infrastructure funds, debt from their credit funds, and sometimes real estate capital (for data centers with power components). This multi-capital approach allows mega-funds to participate in energy transactions at every level of the capital stack and to structure deals that a single-strategy fund cannot.

Blackstone is the archetype: it owns QTS Realty Trust (data centers, $10B take-private), acquired the Potomac Energy Center (774 MW gas plant in Virginia serving data centers, January 2025), and has developed the "Bring Your Own Power" (BYOP) model — delivering purpose-built energy infrastructure directly to data center campuses, bypassing utility grid connection delays. Apollo has an active energy credit strategy (lending against oil and gas reserves, project finance debt, energy infrastructure bridge loans) alongside its equity infrastructure platform. Carlyle has established a clean energy developer and has been active in energy transition M&A.

THE DEAL STRUCTURE TOOLKIT: HOW PE FINANCES ENERGY ASSETS

Energy PE deals require a broader toolkit than traditional buyouts. Understanding each financing instrument — and when it is appropriate — is the core technical competency for any energy PE interview.

1. Non-Recourse Project Finance

The most important financing structure in energy PE. A non-recourse loan is made to a special purpose vehicle (SPV) that owns a single asset (a solar farm, a gas plant, a pipeline). The lender has recourse only to the SPV's assets and cash flows — not to the PE fund or the broader portfolio. This is why "project finance" is distinct from "corporate finance": if the project fails, the lender can seize the asset, but cannot pursue the fund or its other investments.

Why it matters: non-recourse debt allows PE firms to acquire assets at much higher leverage than corporate LBOs, because the debt is sized against the specific, contracted cash flows of the project rather than a company-level EBITDA multiple. A solar farm with a 20-year PPA can support 55–65% non-recourse debt financing against its contracted cash flow. Key metrics: Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR, typically >1.30× minimum for bankable projects), Loan Life Coverage Ratio (LLCR, >1.40×), and Project Life Coverage Ratio (PLCR). The three most important numbers in any project finance deal.

2. Tax Equity — The Unique Energy Finance Layer

Federal tax credits (ITC, PTC, 45Q, 45U) generate significant value for energy projects — but only for entities with sufficient U.S. tax liability to use them. Most PE funds and project developers do not have enough tax appetite. Tax equity investors — primarily large U.S. banks (JPMorgan invests $5–6B/year, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citi) and insurance companies — purchase the right to receive the tax benefits in exchange for capital invested in the project.

Three primary tax equity structures: (1) Partnership Flip: tax equity investor receives 99% of tax benefits until the flip point (when it achieves a target yield, typically 7–10 years), then sponsor's interest flips up to 95%. The most common structure for solar and wind. (2) Sale-Leaseback: developer sells the asset to the tax equity investor and leases it back. Simpler than partnership flip, particularly useful when combined with back-leverage debt. (3) Inverted Lease: tax equity investor leases the project to a developer. Common for wind. Under the IRA, tax equity can now be transferred directly to any U.S. taxpayer — dramatically broadening the market from ~20 active tax equity investors to hundreds of potential buyers. The $30B+ annual transfer market (Crux, 2024) is the biggest structural change in energy finance since the IRA.

3. Transferable Tax Credits: The IRA's Financial Innovation

The IRA's credit transferability provision (Section 6418) allows project owners to sell ITC, PTC, 45Q, 45V, and 45U credits to any U.S. corporation, no partnership required. The buyer pays cash (typically $0.92–$0.97 per $1.00 of credits) and receives a tax deduction. This has created a large, liquid market for tax credits that previously required complex partnership structures. PE firms are participating on both sides: as sellers (monetizing credits from their portfolio projects faster and more flexibly than traditional tax equity allows) and as buyers (purchasing credits at a discount as a high-yield, low-risk investment — essentially buying a guaranteed tax deduction at 3–8 cents on the dollar discount).

4. YieldCos and Continuation Vehicles

A YieldCo is a publicly listed vehicle that holds operating, cash-flow-generating energy assets — initially used by solar and wind developers (SunPower, NextEra, TerraForm Power) to monetize operating assets at lower cost of capital than PE funds. PE firms use YieldCo-like structures (or listed infrastructure vehicles) to access public market capital for their mature infrastructure assets. Brookfield Renewable Partners (NYSE: BEP) and Brookfield Infrastructure Partners (NYSE: BIP) are the most successful examples: publicly listed, distributing regular dividends, trading at infrastructure REIT-like multiples, and providing Brookfield with a permanent capital vehicle alongside its private funds. KKR and Stonepeak have both explored or established similar listed vehicles.

5. Energy Credit and Debt Strategies

PE-affiliated credit strategies have become as significant as equity in energy. Apollo's energy credit arm makes senior secured loans against oil and gas reserves (reserve-based lending, RBL) and infrastructure project finance debt. Ares Infrastructure & Power makes mezzanine and junior infrastructure debt that sits between senior project finance and equity. Blackstone Credit provides bridge financing for energy construction. Stonepeak’s credit platform provides preferred equity and junior capital to energy projects that need more flexible capital than project finance allows. These credit strategies typically target net returns of 8–14% — below equity but with significantly better downside protection — and have attracted large LP allocations from insurance companies seeking yield.

CURRENT THEMES: WHERE PE IS DEPLOYING IN ENERGY (2025–2026)

Six themes are dominating PE energy deal flow in 2025–2026, each driven by the convergence of AI power demand, OBBBA policy realignment, and the structural energy transition.

Theme 1: Data Center Power: The Defining Theme of 2025  Data Center Power: The Defining Theme of 2025

AI data centers need gigawatt-scale firm power, and PE firms are building the energy infrastructure to serve them. Blackstone's BYOP model — acquiring or developing power plants specifically to serve co-located data centers, bypassing utility interconnection queues — is the most innovative energy PE structure of the cycle. Its January 2025 acquisition of the Potomac Energy Center (774 MW gas, Virginia) for data center service is the flagship transaction. Stonepeak operates six major data center platforms (Cologix, CoreSite, Digital Edge, Cirion, Princeton Digital Group, Montera Infrastructure) across 100+ facilities. KKR, Energy Capital Partners, and Bridgepoint have formed a data center infrastructure JV. The PE thesis: data centers are the most creditworthy power customers in history — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are investment-grade, 10+ year contract counterparties who will pay premium prices for reliable power, making the power assets that serve them core infrastructure.

Theme 2: Gas-Fired Generation: The “Bridge” Play  Gas-Fired Generation: The "Bridge" Play

The OBBBA's curtailment of solar and wind ITC/PTC has reduced near-term renewable energy development activity, while AI data center power demand has simultaneously increased the need for dispatchable firm power. The result: natural gas-fired power plants — particularly combined cycle gas turbines (CCGTs) — have been re-rated dramatically upward by PE buyers. PJM capacity prices surged from $29/MW-day to $329/MW-day (the regulatory cap) in 2025–26, directly benefiting PE owners of gas-fired capacity. FTI Consulting's 2025 M&A review noted that "activity in gas-fired generation and behind the meter generation has rapidly increased as utilities cannot incorporate renewables onto the grid fast enough to support the load growth." ArcLight, Energy Capital Partners, and Blackstone are all active gas generation acquirers.

Theme 3: BESS Portfolio Acquisitions  BESS Portfolio Acquisitions

Battery energy storage systems with 10+ year capacity market or tolling agreement contracts are being acquired as infrastructure assets by PE firms who see them as the cleanest expression of the grid reliability theme. The 48E storage ITC (preserved by OBBBA through 2034) makes BESS project finance economics compelling. PE strategy: acquire portfolios of operating or late-stage development BESS assets, consolidate under a platform company, and exit to a larger infrastructure fund or listed vehicle at a premium. Stonepeak, KKR, Brookfield, and ArcLight are all active in BESS platforms. The key diligence question: are the revenues contracted (tolling agreement, capacity market participation) or merchant (spot arbitrage)? Contracted BESS is infrastructure; merchant BESS is energy PE.

Theme 4: Nuclear Restarts and SMR Development  Nuclear Restarts and SMR Development

Nuclear energy's bipartisan policy support (45U preserved through 2034) and Big Tech anchor demand (Microsoft TMI, Meta TerraPower, Google Kairos) have created a compelling PE entry point. Constellation Energy (NYSE: CEG) — the largest U.S. nuclear operator — has seen its stock re-rated significantly. PE is participating primarily through: (1) equity in nuclear operating companies and YieldCos, (2) project finance debt for nuclear restart capital expenditures (the DOE LPO $1B loan for TMI restart is a template), and (3) equity co-investment in SMR development projects where tech company PPAs provide the revenue anchor for project finance. Holtec International's Palisades restart — backed by DOE loan commitments — is a PE-adjacent development where infrastructure capital is playing alongside government programs.

Theme 5: LNG Infrastructure: The Energy Security Play  LNG Infrastructure: The Energy Security Play

U.S. LNG export infrastructure — liquefaction terminals, pipelines, marine loading facilities — has attracted significant PE capital as energy security concerns have elevated LNG to a strategic asset class. Stonepeak's April 2025 partnership with Woodside Energy on the Louisiana LNG project is the most significant recent PE-LNG transaction. The PE thesis: LNG liquefaction terminals with 20+ year offtake agreements from investment-grade European and Asian utilities are infrastructure assets in the most classical sense — essential service, long-duration contracts, inflation-linked revenues. The Trump administration's energy dominance agenda and the Qatar Ras Laffan attack (March 2026) have both elevated the strategic premium on U.S. LNG infrastructure.

Theme 6: Energy Transition Platform Buyouts: The OBBBA Re-Underwriting  Energy Transition Platform Buyouts: The OBBBA Re-Underwriting

The OBBBA's policy changes required PE firms to re-underwrite their energy transition portfolios. The key distinction: assets with commercial economics (grid software, nuclear, storage, geothermal, CCS) retained their investment thesis unchanged; assets dependent on eliminated credits (residential solar, onshore wind development, EV charging networks) required write-downs or strategic pivots. FTI Consulting's 2025 review noted that the OBBBA's Section 163(j) change — reverting to EBITDA standard for interest deductibility — "removes the depreciation penalty that previously constrained capital-intensive borrowers" and "materially expands tax-efficient debt capacity, a structural shift likely to reignite private equity LBO activity" in capital-intensive energy assets. This is the most important OBBBA provision for PE dealmakers that most candidates miss.

THE OBBBA AND PE ENERGY DEAL ACTIVITY

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) is the most important policy event for energy PE since the IRA itself. Its effects on PE deal activity are sector-specific and, in some cases, counterintuitive.

OBBBA Provision

PE Deal Activity Implication

Solar/Wind ITC/PTC Curtailment (phase-out 2027)

Reduces greenfield solar and onshore wind development PE activity. Projects that cannot achieve commercial operation before 2027 face dramatically worse economics. PE firms with large wind/solar development pipelines have re-underwritten or written down assets. Tax equity market for new solar/wind projects is shrinking as the credit phase-out approaches.

48E Storage ITC Preserved (through 2034)

BESS platform acquisitions continue unaffected. PE project finance for storage assets remains fully underwritable with 30% ITC. Stonepeak, ArcLight, and KKR Infrastructure all accelerated BESS platform construction in H2 2025 in response to the OBBBA outcome.

45U Nuclear PTC Preserved (through 2034)

Nuclear operating company equity (Constellation, Vistra) re-rated positively. PE co-investment in nuclear restart projects (Palisades, potential future restarts) has a clear policy anchor. DOE LPO remains active for nuclear project finance alongside PE equity.

45Q CCS Preserved ($85/t geological, $180/t DAC)

Blue hydrogen and industrial CCS project finance deals remain fully underwritable. PE credit strategies (Apollo, Ares) active in CCS project finance debt benefit from preserved economics. ExxonMobil Houston hub discussions involve PE capital alongside oil major balance sheet.

Section 163(j) EBITDA Reversion

The most underappreciated OBBBA provision for PE. By reverting to EBITDA (rather than EBIT) for the interest deductibility cap, OBBBA allows capital-intensive energy borrowers to add back large non-cash depreciation charges when calculating how much interest they can deduct. For a gas plant with significant depreciation, this materially increases tax-efficient debt capacity — directly improving LBO returns. FTI called this a "structural shift likely to reignite private equity LBO activity."

EV Credit Elimination (30D)

EV charging infrastructure PE deals face demand-side headwinds. ChargePoint (CHPT) and EVgo (EVGO) both declined significantly post-OBBBA. PE funds with EV charging platform investments are re-underwriting growth assumptions. Commercial/fleet charging (which never depended on 30D) is less affected than residential or highway consumer charging.

Credit Transferability (Preserved)

The IRA's transferability provision was not touched by OBBBA. The $30B+ annual transferred credit market continues to grow. PE firms that established credit purchase programs in 2023–2024 are continuing to buy transferred ITC/PTC credits from renewable projects as a yield strategy — even as new project development activity slows.

Operational optimization: improve capacity factor from 24% to 26% through inverter upgrades and tracking system optimization (+$3M annual EBITDA)

PPA renegotiation at Year 5: existing PPA has inflation escalator; as market power prices rise with data center demand, the PPA represents below-market pricing — negotiate extension or partial merchant conversion for upside

Refinancing at Year 3: as project de-risks with operating history, refinance senior debt at lower spread (75 bps improvement), releasing ~$15M of equity value

Platform M&A: acquire 2–3 adjacent projects at similar multiples, achieving scale economies in O&M and management costs, qualifying for larger institutional buyer pool at exit

Exit: At Year 6, sell to a listed infrastructure vehicle (Brookfield Renewable Partners or KKR-sponsored listed vehicle) at 15.5× EBITDA on a $60M EBITDA base (after operational improvements). Enterprise value: $930M. Less remaining debt: ~$340M. Equity proceeds: $590M on $324M investment = 1.8× MOIC, ~17.5% levered equity IRR.

CAREERS IN PE ENERGY & INFRASTRUCTURE

Energy and infrastructure PE is one of the most intellectually demanding and financially rewarding career paths in finance — requiring simultaneous fluency in traditional PE mechanics, project finance structures, power market economics, regulatory frameworks, and energy technology.

Infrastructure PE (Dedicated Fund): Infrastructure PE (Dedicated Fund): Analyst/Associate → VP/Principal → Director → MD/Partner at Brookfield, KKR Infrastructure, Stonepeak, EQT, Macquarie. Core competencies: project finance modeling (DSCR, LLCR, PLCR), infrastructure valuation (DCF, regulated rate base, PPP structures), tax equity mechanics, regulatory analysis (FERC, state PUC), and sector expertise. Entry: investment banking (infrastructure/utilities M&A or project finance) + MBA; or engineering + finance.

Energy-Focused Buyout PE: Energy-Focused Buyout PE: Analyst/Associate → VP/Principal at Stonepeak, Energy Capital Partners, ArcLight. Core competencies overlap significantly with infrastructure PE but add traditional LBO modeling, operational value creation, and M&A integration skills. Power market economics (capacity prices, PPA negotiation, merchant revenue forecasting) are the distinguishing technical competency.

Mega-Fund Energy PE (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle): Mega-Fund Energy PE (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle): Infrastructure and energy desks within these platforms. Cross-fund deal flow (equity + credit + real estate) creates more complex deal structures than dedicated infrastructure funds. Compensation at the higher end of PE; advancement highly competitive. Entry: top investment banking analyst program + MBA.

Project Finance / Energy Credit: Project Finance / Energy Credit: Apollo energy credit, Ares Infrastructure debt, Stonepeak credit platform. More lender-oriented than equity PE but increasingly at the center of energy deal activity. Models credit risk rather than equity upside. Entry: project finance banking (Societe Generale, SMBC, ING) or energy-focused law firm + MBA.

Key interview preparation: be ready to walk through the capital stack of a renewable energy project deal from scratch (senior debt, tax equity, equity, and the ITC/PTC role); explain the difference between DSCR and LLCR; describe how the OBBBA has changed PE deal activity by sector; explain what a partnership flip tax equity structure is and why banks are the natural tax equity investors; and articulate which energy assets are "infrastructure PE" vs. "energy buyout PE" and why the return targets differ.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

PE's involvement in energy and infrastructure is in a structurally expansionary phase that will persist regardless of near-term policy or market cycles. The physical requirements of the AI economy — power, transmission, data centers — are irreversible and growing. The energy transition, even with OBBBA headwinds for specific technologies, is creating decades of infrastructure investment opportunity. The question is not whether PE invests in energy — it will — but which strategies, sectors, and structures will generate the best risk-adjusted returns in a world where some IRA subsidies have been removed and others preserved.

Base Case: Base Case: PE energy and infrastructure deal activity grows 10–15% annually through 2028. Data center power platforms become a standard PE asset class. Gas-fired generation remains highly valued as a grid reliability asset. Nuclear restarts attract structured PE co-investment alongside DOE programs. BESS platform consolidation produces several $5–10B+ infrastructure platforms by 2028.

Bull Case: Bull Case: FERC capacity market reform enables larger PE positions in grid infrastructure; SMR commercial deployment creates a new asset class for infrastructure PE; LNG export expansion drives PE acquisition of U.S. midstream; Section 163(j) EBITDA reversion enables larger energy LBOs; PE energy AUM doubles from current levels by 2030.

Bear Case: Bear Case: Interest rates remain elevated at 5%+, compressing infrastructure PE returns below target; grid connection delays reduce BESS and renewable energy deal flow; nuclear restart projects face construction delays; LNG infrastructure faces demand risk as European energy security normalizes; PE energy returns disappoint relative to other alternative strategies.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"The most important thing to understand about PE energy deals vs. traditional LBOs is the tax equity layer. When you buy a solar farm, 30% of your project costs are recoverable through federal tax credits — but only if you have enough U.S. tax liability to use them, which most PE funds don't. So you bring in a bank as a tax equity investor, they contribute capital in exchange for receiving the tax benefits, and they earn their return from the ITC rather than from cash flow. The entire capital stack is different from any other PE deal you've worked on."

"Blackstone's Bring Your Own Power model is the most innovative energy PE structure of the current cycle. Instead of waiting 3–7 years for a utility to connect a new data center to the grid, Blackstone builds or acquires a dedicated power plant and connects it directly to the data center behind the meter. The Potomac Energy Center acquisition — 774 MW of gas generation serving Virginia data centers — is the template. It solves the grid interconnection bottleneck by bypassing the grid entirely."

"The OBBBA provision that most PE investors missed initially is the Section 163(j) EBITDA reversion. For a capital-intensive energy borrower with large non-cash depreciation — a gas plant, a pipeline, a BESS portfolio — reverting to EBITDA rather than EBIT for the interest deductibility cap materially increases the debt you can put on the deal tax-efficiently. FTI called it a structural shift that will reignite LBO activity in capital-intensive energy. It's the best news in OBBBA for PE dealmakers that didn't make any headlines."

"The clearest way I can explain the difference between infrastructure PE and energy buyout PE is to compare the return target to the holding strategy. Infrastructure PE at Brookfield or KKR targets 9–12% unlevered IRR — they buy a contracted pipeline, finance it at infrastructure rates, and earn a yield over 15 years. Energy buyout PE at Stonepeak or Energy Capital Partners targets 18–25% levered equity IRR — they buy a power generation platform, optimize operations, add assets through M&A, and sell to a larger buyer in 5–7 years. Same asset class, completely different game."
