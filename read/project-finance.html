PROJECT FINANCE

MBA Industry Primer | Infrastructure & Energy Finance

Columbia Business School | May 2026

INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Project finance is the specialized financing structure used to fund large infrastructure and energy assets where debt is repaid from the cash flows of the project itself, not from the balance sheet of the sponsoring company. It is the foundational financing mechanism for virtually every major energy, infrastructure, and extractive industry project in the world: solar and wind farms, LNG terminals, toll roads, pipelines, mining projects, hospitals, and now AI data centers. The global project finance market surged to $1.174 trillion in full-year 2025, a 37% increase from 2024, driven by renewables and telecoms. For MBA candidates, project finance literacy is the single most technically differentiating skill in infrastructure and energy investing. Understanding it means understanding DSCR, LLCR, and PLCR — the three credit ratios that determine how much debt a project can support; debt sculpting — how lenders shape the repayment profile to match a project’s cash flow curve; the capital structure waterfall — how senior debt, mezzanine, tax equity, and sponsor equity sit in priority order; the contractual architecture — how EPC, O&M, offtake, and concession agreements allocate risk; and the parties at the table — from commercial bank arrangers to export credit agencies to the DOE Loan Programs Office. No interview at an infrastructure fund, project finance bank, development finance institution, or energy developer can be navigated without this framework.

WHAT MAKES PROJECT FINANCE DIFFERENT FROM CORPORATE FINANCE

Project finance is a fundamentally different financing philosophy from corporate finance. In corporate finance, a company borrows against its balance sheet — lenders assess the total assets, revenues, and creditworthiness of the entire enterprise. In project finance, lenders assess only the specific project — its contracted revenues, its costs, its technology risk, and its regulatory environment. The project is legally "ring-fenced" from its sponsors in a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV). This distinction has enormous implications for how deals are structured, how risk is allocated, and what skills are required.

Dimension

Corporate Finance vs. Project Finance

Debt Recourse

Corporate finance: full recourse to company balance sheet; lenders can pursue all company assets in default. Project finance: limited or non-recourse; lenders’ only recourse is the project assets, contracts, and cash flows — they cannot pursue the sponsor’s other assets

Primary Credit Analysis

Corporate: revenue diversification, balance sheet strength, management track record, industry position. Project: contracted cash flows (offtake agreement quality), construction risk (EPC contractor), technology risk, regulatory risk, reserve and maintenance accounts

Debt Capacity

Corporate: leverage limited by total enterprise cash flows and assets. Project: leverage limited by project’s own CFADS and DSCR covenants; often achieves higher leverage (60–70% LTV) than corporate because contracted cash flows are highly predictable

Interest Rate

Corporate bonds: spreads reflect corporate credit rating (IG: SOFR+50–150bps). Project finance loans: SOFR + 150–300bps for infrastructure; 200–350bps for emerging markets; reflects project risk premium over corporate credit

Tenor

Corporate bonds: typically 5–10 years. Project finance: 15–25 years matching project economics; solar and wind projects routinely have 18–22 year debt tenors to match 20–25 year offtake agreements

Collateral

Corporate: general unsecured claim on company assets. Project finance: specific pledge of project assets (the plant, the land, the equipment), assignment of all contracts (offtake, EPC, O&M, insurance), assignment of project bank accounts, pledge of equity in the SPV

Financial Covenants

Corporate: typically net leverage, interest coverage, EBITDA maintenance. Project finance: DSCR covenant (minimum 1.20–1.25× maintenance), DSRA funding requirements, cash sweep triggers, lock-up provisions, restricted payment conditions

Transaction Complexity

Corporate debt: relatively standardized. Project finance: bespoke; each deal requires negotiating 15–30 distinct contracts (credit agreement, security documents, EPC, O&M, offtake, insurance, direct agreements with each counterparty); a large project can take 12–24 months from mandate to financial close

THE PROJECT FINANCE CAPITAL STRUCTURE

The capital structure of a project finance deal is a precisely engineered hierarchy of claims on the project’s cash flows. Each layer has a defined priority, return expectation, and risk tolerance. Understanding this structure — and how cash flows cascade from the top to the bottom — is the foundation of project finance analysis.

Capital Layer

Return Profile, Priority, Sizing & Key Providers

Senior Secured Debt (55–70%)

Priority: first claim on all cash flows and assets. Return: SOFR + 150–300bps (infrastructure); 200–350bps (power); fixed-rate green bonds at 3–6% for investment-grade projects. Tenor: 15–22 years (matching offtake). Sizing: driven by minimum DSCR (1.25–1.40×) and LLCR (1.30–1.50×) targets. Amortization: sculpted to match CFADS profile. DSRA: typically 6-month forward debt service reserve; required at financial close and maintained throughout tenor. Key providers: commercial bank syndicates (MUFG, SocGen, ING, BNP, SMBC, Natixis), green bonds (for investment-grade projects), export credit agencies (ECA: JBIC, UK Export Finance, US EXIM), multilateral development banks (World Bank IFC, EBRD, ADB, IDB).

Mezzanine / Subordinated Debt (5–15%)

Priority: subordinated to senior; paid after senior debt service. Return: SOFR + 400–700bps; often partially PIK (payment-in-kind, accruing). Tenor: typically matches senior tenor or slightly shorter. Use case: bridge the gap between senior debt capacity and available equity; used in transactions where DSCR constraint limits senior debt but the sponsor wants higher leverage. Key providers: infrastructure debt funds (BlackRock Infrastructure Debt, Macquarie Infrastructure Debt, Brookfield Credit), mezzanine-specialized boutiques, institutional investors seeking yield above IG. Intercreditor agreement: governs relationship between senior and mezz; standstill provisions typically prevent mezz from accelerating for 90–180 days after senior default.

Tax Equity (10–20%, U.S. Renewables Only)

Priority: complex; structured as partnership with special allocation of tax credits and depreciation. Return: 7–9% gross all-in yield from combination of tax credit value and cash distributions; tax equity investor’s primary "return" is the tax shield, not cash. Mechanism: the project is structured as a tax equity partnership (typically "partnership flip" structure); tax equity investor receives 99% of the project’s tax credits and depreciation until it achieves its target yield, then flips to a small minority interest (5–10%). Key investors: JPMorgan Chase ($4–6B/year), Bank of America ($3–5B/year), US Bancorp, Wells Fargo, Berkshire Hathaway Energy. OBBBA impact: preserved 48E storage ITC and 45U nuclear PTC; curtailed but preserved 45Q CCS; eliminated 30D EV and 25D residential solar — tax equity market shrinks for eliminated credits.

Sponsor Equity (20–35%)

Priority: last in (residual claimant after all other obligations). Return: target equity IRR 10–15% for operating infrastructure; 15–25% for development-stage assets; measured as unlevered IRR on project and levered IRR on equity contribution. Contributions: development costs (paid pre-financial close from sponsor’s own capital); construction equity (funded as construction draw schedule requires, often after debt is drawn pro-rata); invested as equity in the SPV. Restricted payments: project typically cannot distribute cash to sponsor equity until: DSCR is above maintenance threshold (often 1.20×); DSRA is fully funded; no event of default exists. Key providers: infrastructure PE (Brookfield, KKR, Macquarie, Stonepeak, Global Infrastructure Partners), IPPs (NextEra, AES, Orsted), industrial sponsors (utilities, oil companies), sovereign wealth funds (GIC, ADIA, Mubadala) as co-investors.

THE CORE CREDIT RATIOS: DSCR, LLCR, AND PLCR

The three coverage ratios — DSCR, LLCR, and PLCR — are the quantitative backbone of project finance credit analysis. They translate a project’s cash flow forecast into the debt capacity the project can support. Understanding them conceptually and being able to calculate them from a model is the single most important technical skill in project finance.

CFADS: The Foundation of Everything

Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS) is the central metric in project finance modeling. Everything else flows from it. CFADS = Revenue – Operating Costs – Maintenance Capex +/– Changes in Working Capital – Cash Taxes. It is the cash actually available to pay lenders, after all operating expenses but before any debt service. The distinction from EBITDA: CFADS subtracts maintenance capex and working capital changes that EBITDA ignores, and subtracts cash taxes (not accounting taxes). In a solar project, CFADS is relatively stable and predictable; in a toll road, it follows traffic growth; in an LNG project, it reflects the take-or-pay structure of the offtake contract.

DSCR: The Operating Covenant

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) = CFADS in one period / Debt Service in the same period. Debt Service = scheduled principal repayment + interest expense. A DSCR of 1.30× means the project generates $1.30 of cash for every $1.00 of debt service — providing a 30% cushion against revenue shortfalls or cost overruns. The minimum DSCR at any point in the loan life is set in the term sheet: typically 1.25–1.30× for investment-grade infrastructure (solar, wind, regulated utilities), 1.30–1.40× for merchant or higher-risk assets. If the DSCR drops below the maintenance threshold (often 1.20×), the project triggers a covenant breach: distributions to equity are blocked, the DSRA may be drawn, and lenders may declare an event of default if not remedied.

Debt Sculpting: Matching Repayment to Cash Flows

Debt sculpting is the process of shaping each period’s principal repayment so that DSCR remains at or above the target throughout the loan life. In a conventional corporate loan, repayments follow a fixed schedule (annuity or bullet). In project finance, because CFADS typically follows a project-specific curve (a solar project’s CFADS declines slightly over time due to panel degradation; a toll road’s increases as traffic grows), a flat repayment schedule would result in high DSCR early and potentially sub-threshold DSCR late in the project life. Sculpting solves this: the lender sizes each period’s maximum allowable debt service as CFADS / target DSCR, then backs out the principal repayment as (max debt service – interest expense). This creates a repayment profile that precisely matches the project’s cash flow shape, maximizing debt capacity while maintaining the coverage covenant throughout.

LLCR: Sizing Debt at Financial Close

Loan Life Coverage Ratio (LLCR) = Present Value of all future CFADS within the loan tenor / Current Outstanding Debt Balance. Unlike DSCR (which is a point-in-time measure), LLCR takes a forward-looking view across the entire remaining loan life, discounted at the cost of debt. Initial LLCR = PV of all projected CFADS during the loan term / Initial Debt Balance. Target at financial close: 1.30–1.50× for infrastructure. Relationship to DSCR: when debt is sculpted based on a constant target DSCR, the initial LLCR equals the DSCR target — they are mathematically equivalent under those conditions. In practice, lenders use both: DSCR for ongoing covenant monitoring (what is the project doing now?) and LLCR for initial sizing (what is the maximum debt the project can support?). The debt sizing equation: Initial Debt = PV of projected CFADS over loan life / target LLCR.

PLCR: The Lenders’ Comfort Margin

Project Life Coverage Ratio (PLCR) = Present Value of all future CFADS over the entire project life (not just the loan life) / Current Outstanding Debt Balance. PLCR extends the analysis beyond the loan maturity to include the "merchant tail" — the period when the project continues operating but has no debt service. A project with a 15-year loan but a 30-year operating life has a significant merchant tail. Lenders care about PLCR because it confirms that even if the project underperforms during the loan life, the long-term asset value provides additional security. Target PLCR at financial close: 1.60–2.0× for investment-grade assets. PLCR > LLCR > DSCR always (PLCR has the longest time horizon; DSCR has the shortest). Summary: DSCR = "can the project pay its bills this year?"; LLCR = "can the project repay all its debt?"; PLCR = "what is the total value of the asset relative to outstanding debt?".

THE CONTRACTUAL ARCHITECTURE: HOW RISK IS ALLOCATED

Project finance is fundamentally a risk allocation exercise. Lenders will only lend against a project’s cash flows if they are confident those cash flows are predictable and contractually supported. The contractual architecture — the suite of agreements that govern the project — allocates every material risk to the party best positioned to manage or bear it. Understanding these contracts is the other half of project finance technical literacy.

Contract / Document

Risk Allocated, Key Terms & Lender Requirements

Offtake Agreement (PPA, Concession, Tolling)

The most important document in any project finance deal. Defines how the project earns revenue: Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) for energy projects; concession agreement for toll roads, airports, ports; tolling agreement for pipelines and LNG facilities; take-or-pay for mining off-take. Key lender requirements: investment-grade counterparty; term matching or exceeding loan tenor; fixed or formula-driven pricing (not fully merchant); termination payment provisions adequate to repay outstanding debt in the event of early termination. The PPA is why a solar project can borrow at 60–70% LTV with 18-year tenor: lenders are lending against contracted cash flows, not speculative merchant revenues.

EPC Contract (Engineering, Procurement, Construction)

Allocates construction risk to the EPC contractor. Key provisions: fixed price and fixed schedule (lump sum, date certain EPC — the gold standard for project finance); liquidated damages for delay (LD payments covering debt service during construction delay); performance LDs for technology shortfalls (e.g., solar panel underperformance vs. P50 estimate); payment and performance bonds (EPC contractor’s surety backs its obligations). Lender requirement: EPC contractor must be financially capable of paying LDs; credit support (parent guarantee, bond) required. A "contractor-financed" or "cost-plus" EPC structure is significantly less lendable than lump sum date-certain.

O&M Agreement (Operations & Maintenance)

Governs post-construction operations. Key provisions: fixed annual fee or cost-plus structure; availability and performance guarantees; rectification obligations for underperformance; insurance requirements. Long-term O&M from the OEM (wind turbine manufacturer, solar panel manufacturer) provides lenders additional comfort on technology performance over the asset’s life. Key risk: operations cost overruns that compress CFADS below DSCR covenant.

Fuel Supply / Resource Agreement

Allocates fuel or resource supply risk. For gas-fired plants: long-term gas supply agreement with creditworthy counterparty at fixed or formula-linked price; if gas supply is merchant, lenders require detailed sensitivity analysis. For wind/solar: resource assessment (P50/P90/P99 energy yield studies by independent engineer); basis risk between generation forecast and actual grid dispatch. For LNG: feed gas supply contract and reservoir risk; independent reservoir engineer certification of reserves.

Interconnection Agreement (Energy Projects)

The project’s legal right to connect to the transmission grid and export power. A critical and increasingly problematic document given the U.S. interconnection queue crisis: projects with interconnection agreements are bankable; projects still in the queue are not. FERC Order 2023 cluster study reforms are attempting to streamline interconnection, but an executable interconnection agreement remains a prerequisite for project finance close.

Direct Agreements

Bilateral agreements between lenders and each key counterparty (EPC contractor, O&M provider, offtaker, government authority) giving lenders the right to "step in" and take over the contract if the project defaults. Direct agreements are what give lenders meaningful security over project contracts — without them, contract termination in a default scenario destroys the project’s value as a going concern. Negotiating direct agreements with each counterparty (who resist giving lenders rights over their contracts) is among the most time-consuming parts of project finance due diligence.

Intercreditor Agreement

Governs the relationship between senior lenders and any subordinated creditors (mezzanine, second lien). Key provisions: payment subordination (sub debt cannot receive payments if senior DSCR is below threshold); enforcement standstill (sub creditors cannot accelerate or enforce for 90–180 days after senior event of default); voting rights in restructuring. The intercreditor agreement is what makes the senior/mezz capital stack work — it defines how the two creditor groups interact in normal operation and in distress.

Security Package

The totality of collateral pledged to lenders: (1) mortgage/charge over project land and physical assets; (2) assignment of all project contracts (offtake, EPC, O&M, fuel supply, insurance); (3) pledge of shares in the SPV (lenders can take ownership of the project company); (4) charge over all project bank accounts (including DSRA, MMRA, distribution account); (5) assignment of insurance proceeds; (6) direct agreements with counterparties. In a default, lenders can enforce the security package to take control of the project as a going concern and continue operating it.

Debt Service Reserve Account (DSRA)

A bank account holding 6 months of forward debt service (or more, depending on lender requirements). Funded at financial close and maintained throughout the loan life. If CFADS is insufficient to meet a scheduled payment, the DSRA is drawn to make up the shortfall — providing a liquidity buffer against short-term cash flow volatility. Replenished from subsequent cash flows before any distribution to equity. The DSRA is the first line of defense against covenant breach; its size reflects lenders’ assessment of cash flow volatility risk.

THE PROJECT FINANCE LIFECYCLE

A typical infrastructure project finance transaction moves through six phases from concept to financial close, each with distinct activities, participants, and deliverables. Understanding this lifecycle is essential for any role in project development, banking, or investment.

Phase

Timeline, Key Activities & Participants

1. Development & Origination

12–48 months pre-financial close. Sponsor activities: site acquisition, resource assessment (wind/solar), permitting (NEPA, state environmental, local zoning), interconnection application, technology selection, offtake solicitation (RFP, bilateral negotiation). Key costs: development capex of $5–50M that is at-risk until financial close. Key parties: sponsor development team, permitting counsel, environmental consultants, resource assessors (DNV, UL, AWS Truepower), interconnection consultants. Key milestone: interconnection agreement executed and EPC contract negotiated.

2. Mandate and Structuring

3–6 months. Sponsor selects lead arranger(s) based on bank pitches; mandate letter signed. Bank structures the deal: debt sizing (DSCR/LLCR analysis), capital structure determination, key term negotiation (pricing, tenor, covenants, security). Independent engineer, insurance advisor, and legal counsel engaged. Financial model built: 25–30-year operational model with CFADS, DSCR, LLCR, PLCR, equity IRR. Key deliverables: information memorandum (IM) distributed to syndication banks.

3. Due Diligence

3–6 months. Lenders conduct technical, legal, insurance, environmental, and financial due diligence in parallel. Independent engineer reviews EPC contract, energy yield assessment, O&M plan, and technology risk. Insurance advisor reviews coverage adequacy. Legal counsel reviews all project contracts, permits, land rights, and regulatory approvals. Environmental and social impact assessment for projects with MDB participation (IFC Performance Standards, Equator Principles). Any material issue identified in DD must be resolved before financial close.

4. Syndication

2–3 months. Lead arranger syndicates the debt to a club of lenders (5–25 banks for large transactions); smaller transactions may stay with 2–3 banks. Lenders review IM, financial model, and DD reports; conduct own analysis; submit commitments. For transactions with ECA participation (JBIC, UK Export Finance, EXIM), ECA due diligence runs in parallel and may be on the critical path. Green / sustainability-linked loan structure determined if applicable.

5. Documentation & Financial Close

3–6 months. Legal teams negotiate and execute 15–30+ transaction documents: credit agreement, security documents, direct agreements with each counterparty, intercreditor agreement, hedging agreements, accounts agreement. Conditions precedent (CP) list: typically 50–100 items that must all be satisfied before the first loan drawdown. Financial close is achieved when all CPs are met and lenders fund the initial draw. For a $1B+ transaction, financial close involves simultaneous execution of hundreds of pages of documents and the movement of hundreds of millions of dollars.

6. Construction, Operations & Refinancing

Construction: 1–3 years; drawdowns on construction facility as project milestones hit; lender’s technical advisor monitors construction progress. Commercial Operation Date (COD): project begins revenue generation; construction loan typically converts to term loan. Operations: DSCR monitored semi-annually; distributions to equity when conditions met; DSRA maintained; reserve accounts funded. Refinancing: many projects refinance at COD or 2–3 years post-COD to access lower operating-stage pricing (construction risk premium removed); green bonds used for qualifying assets; IFC green bonds, green sukuk in Middle East. Secondary market: operating assets sold to infrastructure PE or pension funds seeking stable yield.

TAX EQUITY: THE UNIQUELY AMERICAN FINANCING MECHANISM

Tax equity is a financing structure that exists only in the United States, created by the interaction of U.S. tax law and the structure of energy project partnerships. It enables projects to monetize federal tax credits (ITC and PTC) that the project itself cannot use — because the project has no taxable income — by selling those credits to large financial institutions with substantial U.S. tax liability.

The Partnership Flip Structure

The most common tax equity structure for solar and wind projects is the "partnership flip." The project is owned by a partnership with two partners: the tax equity investor (typically a bank) and the project sponsor. Pre-flip: the tax equity investor receives 99% of the project’s tax credits, depreciation benefits, and cash distributions; the sponsor receives 1%. Post-flip: once the tax equity investor achieves its target yield (typically 7–9% gross all-in), the allocation "flips" — the tax equity investor drops to a 5% interest, the sponsor rises to 95%. The tax equity investor’s return comes primarily from the tax shield value (the present value of the tax credits and depreciation allocations) rather than cash. A $100M solar project might generate $30–40M of ITC tax credits; the tax equity investor pays $25–35M for the right to those credits (a slight discount reflecting time value and transaction costs).

OBBBA Impact on Tax Equity (July 2025)  OBBBA Impact on Tax Equity

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s changes to IRA clean energy credits directly affect the tax equity market: (1) 30D EV credit terminated — no more consumer EV tax equity. (2) 25D residential solar terminated — residential solar tax equity market eliminated. (3) 45Y/48E wind and solar curtailed — must begin construction by July 4, 2026, and be in service by December 31, 2027 to qualify; dramatically shrinks the future tax equity market for new wind and solar. (4) 48E storage ITC preserved through 2032 — battery storage tax equity market continues unimpaired; JPMorgan and BofA see this as a growth segment. (5) 45U nuclear PTC preserved through 2034 — nuclear tax equity is a growing category as Constellation and Vistra pursue nuclear uprates. (6) Transferability (Section 6418) preserved — this is critical: the $20–25B annual transferable credit market allows any U.S. taxpayer to buy tax credits directly, without the complex partnership flip structure. This democratizes tax credit monetization and reduces the dominance of the 20 traditional tax equity banks.

Entry for MBAs: 2–3 years in corporate lending or M&A + MBA, or direct lateral from infrastructure PE or developer. Entry: 2+ years in infrastructure banking or project finance + MBA; lateral from consulting (energy practice) + MBA. Key distinction: infrastructure PE uses project finance on assets it owns; project finance banking structures the debt for others.

Development Finance Institutions: Development Finance Institutions: DOE LPO, World Bank IFC, EBRD, ADB, DFC, JBIC all hire MBAs for investment officer, credit analyst, and portfolio management roles. DFI roles involve: project appraisal (technical, financial, environmental/social), structuring blended finance solutions, portfolio monitoring of operating assets. IFC and EBRD have specific MBA recruitment programs. Policy-oriented MBAs with finance backgrounds are well-positioned; prior consulting or banking experience preferred.

Energy Project Development: Energy Project Development: IPPs (NextEra Energy Resources, Orsted, AES, Invenergy), utilities (Dominion, Eversource), and oil companies (Shell, TotalEnergies) employ MBAs in project development and finance roles that interface directly with project finance banks. Key responsibilities: financial modeling for investment decisions, offtake negotiation, arranging project finance mandates, tax equity structuring. Corporate development roles at IPPs are an excellent entry point for MBAs combining energy interest with finance skills.

Key financial modeling concepts every candidate should be able to walk through: (1) Building a CFADS bridge from EBITDA (what gets added back or subtracted); (2) Sizing debt using the LLCR method step-by-step; (3) Sculpting a debt repayment schedule given a CFADS profile and target DSCR; (4) Calculating equity IRR given a levered cash flow model; (5) Partnership flip mechanics (pre-flip and post-flip allocations); (6) Sensitivity analysis on P50 vs. P90 energy yield; (7) DSRA sizing and impact on equity distributions. If you can walk through all seven in an interview, you will be differentiated from nearly every other MBA candidate in infrastructure finance.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

Project finance is in a structural growth period driven by three converging forces: the energy transition’s capital intensity ($3.5T per year globally in energy investment required by 2030, IEA), AI data center infrastructure buildout ($355B+ hyperscaler capex in 2025 alone), and emerging market infrastructure deficits ($7T+ gap in developing country infrastructure by 2030). These forces are driving both higher volumes and new asset classes.

Base Case: Base Case: Global PF volume stabilizes at $900B–1.1T annually through 2028; renewables remain the dominant sector; data centers emerge as a distinct $100B+ annual PF asset class; SMR nuclear project finance begins with first commercial SMR closes 2028–2030; DOE LPO continues as critical first-mover for first-of-a-kind technologies; tax equity market shrinks post-OBBBA for solar/wind but grows for storage and nuclear; Equator Principles banks increasingly apply IFC Performance Standards to OECD projects.

Bull Case: Bull Case: Permitting reform accelerates U.S. renewable and infrastructure development; SMR project finance demonstrates replicable template enabling 20+ SMR deals by 2032; green bond market deepens, providing lower-cost capital for investment-grade operating assets; emerging market infrastructure finance expands with MDB guarantee programs reducing first-loss risk for commercial banks; data center PF becomes routinely structured alongside behind-the-meter power generation.

Bear Case: Bear Case: Higher-for-longer interest rates reduce project finance economics (fewer projects meet DSCR hurdles at elevated SOFR base); permitting delays extend development timelines and increase at-risk development capital; OBBBA curtailment of 45Y/48E creates uncertainty that delays renewables financial close; geopolitical fragmentation reduces multilateral ECA cooperation; climate-related physical risk (floods, hurricanes, heat) increases insurance costs and reduces CFADS predictability.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"The DSCR is not just a number — it’s the governing constraint on everything in project finance. Once you set the target DSCR at 1.30×, you’ve determined the maximum debt service in every period, which determines the debt balance at financial close, which determines the equity required, which determines the equity IRR. The entire deal flows from that one covenant. And sculpting is what makes it work: instead of forcing a flat repayment schedule onto a project whose cash flows decline over time, you shape each payment to keep DSCR exactly at target throughout the loan life. That maximizes debt capacity while protecting the lender’s cushion in every year."

"The DOE Loan Programs Office is the most underrated institution in U.S. energy finance. It has $400B+ in loan authority and a mandate to finance first-of-a-kind clean energy technologies that commercial banks won’t touch. Three Mile Island restart wouldn’t have happened without DOE LPO. Rivian’s factory wouldn’t have gotten built on the timeline it did without DOE LPO. TerraPower’s Natrium reactor has DOE LPO backing. The LPO is the first-mover that de-risks the technology for subsequent commercial bank participation. Every major clean energy first-of-a-kind deal in the U.S. has a DOE LPO fingerprint on it."

"Project finance is fundamentally different from corporate finance in one way that matters more than all the others: the debt is non-recourse. If a solar project has a bad year and misses DSCR, the bank can’t go after the sponsor’s other assets. It can only draw on the DSRA, block distributions to equity, and ultimately take control of the project. That ring-fence structure is why project finance can achieve 65–70% leverage on a long-dated asset — higher than most LBOs — because the contracted cash flows are more predictable than most corporate EBITDA. The price of that ring-fence is the contractual architecture: you need an EPC contractor taking construction risk, an offtaker with investment-grade credit taking revenue risk, and an O&M provider taking operational risk. The deal is essentially a legal structure for moving risk from the project to parties better positioned to manage it."

"The Lancium $8.1 billion data center project financing in May 2025 was the moment project finance went mainstream for AI infrastructure. That’s a larger single deal than most solar or wind farms. It used the same techniques — DSCR-based debt sizing, long-term contracted revenues, independent technical advisor, DSRA — that project finance banks have used on power plants for 40 years. The key insight: a data center with long-term service contracts from investment-grade customers looks exactly like a power plant with long-term PPAs. If the cash flows are contracted and predictable, project finance can leverage them, regardless of whether the asset generates electricity or processes AI queries."
