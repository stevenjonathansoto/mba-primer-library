INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) is simultaneously the most controversial and most indispensable technology in the global decarbonization toolkit. Indispensable because the IEA, IPCC, and every credible net-zero pathway requires capturing billions of tonnes of CO₂ annually from hard-to-abate industries and the atmosphere — there is no net-zero scenario without CCUS. Controversial because it has been promised for 30 years at costs that never quite fall far enough, in an industry skeptical that it's anything more than a lifeline for fossil fuels. For MBA candidates, CCUS sits at the intersection of industrial decarbonization, infrastructure finance, carbon markets, and one of the most complex regulatory incentive structures in clean energy — the IRA's Section 45Q tax credit.

HISTORY & ORIGINS

Carbon capture is not a new concept — CO₂ has been separated from natural gas streams industrially since the 1930s, and CO₂ injection for Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) began in the 1970s. The Sleipner natural gas field in Norway, operated by Equinor since 1996, was the world's first dedicated geological CO₂ storage project — injecting 1 million tonnes of CO₂ per year into a saline aquifer beneath the North Sea to avoid Norway's carbon tax. But the modern CCUS industry, positioned explicitly as a climate solution, only began gathering momentum with the IEA's 2005 Special Report on Carbon Capture and Storage.

2005–2015: Demonstration Projects and Early Failures.  The IEA's 2005 report established the scientific foundation for large-scale CCS. Governments invested billions in demonstration projects: the EU's Zero Emissions Platform, the U.S. FutureGen program (cancelled 2015 after $1B+ spent), Australia's Gorgon CCS (Chevron, persistent underperformance vs. targets). The fundamental challenge emerged: CCS adds 15–25% to the cost of electricity from power plants, and no carbon price was high enough to make it economic. Most demonstration projects were cancelled or underperformed, and private capital largely avoided the sector.

2016–2021: The Policy Foundation.  The Paris Agreement's net-zero ambitions and the IPCC's 2018 Special Report on 1.5°C both identified CCS as essential — not optional — for reaching net-zero. Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) and Direct Air Capture (DAC) received specific attention as sources of net-negative emissions needed to compensate for hard-to-abate sectors. The U.S. Section 45Q tax credit was significantly expanded by the FUTURE Act (2018): $50/tonne for geologic storage, $35/tonne for EOR. Canada, the UK, and Norway accelerated their CCS strategies. Boundary Dam Unit 3 (SaskPower, Canada) became the first commercial post-combustion CCS coal power plant.

2022–2026: The IRA Inflection and Industrial-Scale Buildout.  The IRA (August 2022) transformed 45Q: $85/tonne for geologic storage, $60/tonne for EOR, $180/tonne for DAC — with a 12-year credit window. ExxonMobil's $4.9B acquisition of Denbury Resources (2023) — the largest CO₂ pipeline network in the U.S. — signaled a major integrated oil company's commitment to CCS as a core business. Occidental's Stratos DAC plant (Permian Basin) became the world's largest operational DAC project in 2024. Norway's Northern Lights (Equinor/Shell/TotalEnergies JV) became the world's first commercial offshore CO₂ shipping and storage project. Australia's Moomba CCS (Santos) began injecting CO₂ into the Cooper Basin in October 2024.

MARKET STRUCTURE & VALUE CHAIN

The CCUS value chain has four major segments: CO₂ capture (at the emission source or directly from air), compression and transport (CO₂ pipeline infrastructure), storage (geological sequestration or utilization), and monitoring/verification (ensuring permanence of storage). Each segment has distinct technology, capital, and regulatory requirements.

The CCUS Value Chain

1. CO₂ Source & Capture Technology

Post-combustion (flue gas scrubbing with amine solvents — most common; retrofittable to existing plants); Pre-combustion (converts fuel to H₂ + CO₂ before combustion); Oxy-fuel (burns fuel in pure O₂, producing concentrated CO₂); Direct Air Capture (DAC: fans draw ambient air over adsorbent material; energy-intensive but location-flexible). Capture efficiency: 85–95% of CO₂ from point sources.

2. CO₂ Compression

Captured CO₂ must be compressed to supercritical phase (~73 bar) for pipeline transport. Compression requires significant electricity (15–30% of captured energy value). Key suppliers: MAN Energy Solutions, Atlas Copco, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy.

3. CO₂ Pipeline Transport

Supercritical CO₂ transported via dedicated pipeline to storage site. Existing U.S. CO₂ pipeline network: ~5,000 miles (ExxonMobil/Denbury dominant). CO₂ pipelines require different materials than natural gas pipelines (risk of stress corrosion cracking). Midwest carbon capture networks (Summit Carbon Solutions, Navigator) facing permitting challenges. Key bottleneck.

4. Geological Storage

CO₂ injected into deep geological formations: saline aquifers (most abundant globally), depleted oil/gas reservoirs, basalt formations (rapid mineralization; used by Carbfix in Iceland). Saline aquifers in the U.S. Gulf Coast and North Sea have enormous capacity. Monitoring required for 50–100 years post-injection to verify permanence.

5. Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR)

CO₂ injected into oil reservoirs to increase oil recovery (~15–25% more oil). The dominant CCUS use case today: U.S. Permian Basin uses ~60 Mtpa CO₂-EOR. The 45Q credit ($60/tonne for EOR) incentivizes capturing CO₂ for EOR vs. venting. Controversial: produces more oil, which when burned emits more CO₂.

6. CO₂ Utilization

Converting CO₂ into useful products: synthetic fuels (e-fuels), concrete and building materials (CarbonCure, Heirloom), chemicals, and beverages. Limited commercial scale today but growing. "Carbon-to-value" startups are an emerging VC category. Products must be durable (permanent CO₂ sequestration) or themselves produce avoided emissions.

7. Monitoring, Reporting & Verification (MRV)

Tracking and verifying CO₂ storage permanence over decades. Seismic surveys, pressure monitoring, satellite observation (GHGSat, Carbon Mapper). EPA Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI wells govern U.S. geological storage. IEA-GHG monitoring protocols for international projects. MRV quality determines credit quality for voluntary carbon markets.

Three market segments define CCUS:

Point Source CCS (Dominant Segment): Capturing CO₂ at large industrial emission sources — power plants, cement kilns, steel furnaces, chemical plants, ethanol facilities. Post-combustion capture with amine scrubbing is the most common technology. Carbon intensity reduction: 85–95%. Economic viability requires carbon prices or credits above ~$50–85/tonne, which the IRA 45Q now provides in the U.S.

Direct Air Capture (DAC): Removing CO₂ directly from the ambient atmosphere using sorbent fans and chemicals. Energy-intensive ($400–600/tonne current cost; DOE $100/tonne target by 2035). Location-flexible. The IRA provides $180/tonne 45Q credit — the highest incentive in U.S. clean energy policy. Occidental's Stratos (0.5 Mtpa) and Climeworks' Mammoth (Iceland, 36,000 tpa) are the leading commercial plants. Critical for generating "negative emissions" to compensate for residual hard-to-abate sectors.

Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS): Growing biomass (which absorbs CO₂) then burning it for energy and capturing the resulting CO₂ produces net-negative emissions. Drax Power (UK) is the flagship BECCS project (converting coal plant to biomass + CCS). BECCS is the primary negative emissions technology in most IPCC net-zero scenarios but faces land use, sustainability, and water concerns at scale.

Carbon Markets & Financial Players

CCUS intersects with both compliance and voluntary carbon markets. Compliance markets: EU ETS (€60–80/tonne CO₂) creates direct financial incentive for industrial CCS in Europe. California Cap-and-Trade ($30/tonne CO₂), UK ETS, and Canada's OBPS all provide compliance value for captured emissions. Voluntary carbon markets: High-quality, permanent geological storage CO₂ removal credits (from DAC or BECCS) are the premium category — Microsoft, Stripe, and Shopify pay $400–1,000/tonne for verified permanent removal. Infrastructure funds: Brookfield, BlackRock, and KKR are evaluating CO₂ pipeline and storage infrastructure as a regulated infrastructure asset class. The analogy to natural gas pipelines (regulated rate of return, long-term contracts) makes CO₂ transport and storage attractive for infrastructure capital.

ANATOMY OF A CCUS FACILITY

A CCUS facility captures CO₂ at a point source, compresses it for transport, and injects it into a geological storage formation. Understanding each component is essential for evaluating technology risk, capital cost, and regulatory requirements.

Component

Key Specs

Strategic Significance

Capture Unit (Post-Combustion)

Amine scrubbing column; MEA, MDEA, or proprietary solvents; 30–40m tall absorber column

85–95% CO₂ capture efficiency from flue gas. Amine solvent regeneration consumes 15–25% of plant's energy — the "energy penalty" that reduces efficiency and increases cost. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (KS-21), Shell Cansolv, Fluor Econamine are leading technologies.

Direct Air Capture Unit

Modular contactors; liquid or solid sorbents; 400°C regeneration temperature (solid); vacuum swing (solid)

DAC requires 1,400–2,000 kWh/tonne CO₂ vs. ~150 kWh/tonne for post-combustion — the energy intensity is why cost is so high. Renewable-powered DAC is the target. Climeworks solid sorbent vs. Carbon Engineering liquid solvent — two leading technology pathways.

CO₂ Drying & Compression

Dehydration to <50 ppm H₂O; multi-stage centrifugal compression to 100–150 bar; supercritical phase

Supercritical CO₂ (above 73 bar, 31°C) behaves like a dense fluid — easier to pipeline and inject. Free water causes corrosion in CO₂ pipelines. Compression is energy-intensive.

CO₂ Pipeline

Carbon steel pipeline; 6–24 inch diameter; 100–150 bar operating pressure; corrosion-resistant lining

CO₂ pipelines must be different from natural gas pipelines — dry CO₂ is safe, but wet CO₂ forms carbonic acid and corrodes steel. Special valve and monitoring requirements. ExxonMobil/Denbury's 1,300-mile U.S. network is the largest.

Injection Well

Class VI injection well (EPA UIC); 1,000–3,000m depth; perforated casing for CO₂ injection

Class VI permitting is the primary regulatory bottleneck in the U.S. — EPA has issued fewer than 20 Class VI permits; 130+ pending. Permitting timeline: 2–7 years. Wyoming and North Dakota have primacy authority, enabling faster state-level permitting.

Storage Formation

Saline aquifer, depleted reservoir, or basalt; 500+ meters of caprock above storage zone

CO₂ trapping mechanisms: structural (caprock), dissolution, mineral (converts to carbonate over centuries), residual (small bubbles trapped in pore spaces). Mineral trapping in basalt (Carbfix) is the most permanent. Saline aquifers in the U.S. Gulf Coast have capacity for 500+ years of U.S. CO₂ emissions.

Monitoring, Reporting & Verification (MRV)

Seismic surveys, pressure monitoring, groundwater sampling, satellite InSAR, microseismic monitoring

MRV is required by EPA UIC regulations and is the basis for 45Q credit claims. Continuous monitoring required during injection; periodic monitoring for 50+ years post-injection. GHGSat (satellite methane/CO₂ monitoring) and Carbon Mapper are key technology providers.

How it works: At the emission source, CO₂-containing flue gas (typically 3–15% CO₂ by volume) contacts an amine solvent in an absorber column; the amine chemically binds CO₂ while letting other gases pass through. The CO₂-rich amine is heated in a stripper column, releasing concentrated CO₂ (>99%) and regenerating the solvent. The captured CO₂ is then dried, compressed to supercritical phase, and transported via pipeline to the injection site. Injection wells push the supercritical CO₂ into permeable geological formations below an impermeable caprock. Over time, CO₂ dissolves in brine (dissolution trapping), reacts with minerals (mineral trapping), or remains trapped as a free phase under the caprock (structural trapping). Well-designed storage is permanent on geological timescales.

GLOBAL GEOGRAPHY & COUNTRY LEADERS

United States:  The global leader in CCUS investment, driven by the IRA's 45Q credit. North America holds ~37% of global CCUS market share and hosts the largest CO₂ pipeline network (ExxonMobil/Denbury, Kinder Morgan CO₂). The Gulf Coast (Texas, Louisiana) has the best combination of industrial emitters, CO₂ pipeline infrastructure, and geological storage capacity in the world. Permian Basin CO₂-EOR is the largest CCUS market segment. DOE's 7 Regional H₂ Hubs include CCUS components. Primary regulatory bottleneck: EPA Class VI injection well permitting (2–7 years).

Norway:  The most advanced offshore CO₂ storage ecosystem. Sleipner (since 1996) and Snøhvit have stored 35+ million tonnes cumulatively. Northern Lights — the first commercial CO₂ shipping, storage, and transport service — became operational in 2025, accepting CO₂ captured by European industrial emitters (Heidelberg Materials Brevik cement plant was first customer). The Norwegian continental shelf has enormous storage capacity — estimated 70+ Gt CO₂.

United Kingdom:  Ambitious CCUS strategy centered on two industrial clusters: HyNet North West (Liverpool-Manchester) and East Coast Cluster (Teesside-Humber). Government committed £21.7B to CCUS industrial decarbonization. Drax BECCS is the flagship project. North Sea geological storage is the target. Track-1 projects (HyNet, East Coast) are at Final Investment Decision stage.

Australia:  Santos's Moomba CCS (Cooper Basin, South Australia) became operational October 2024 — injecting 1.7 million tonnes CO₂/year from the Moomba gas plant into the Patchawarra aquifer. Chevron's Gorgon CCS (LNG project, WA) has underperformed targets but demonstrated the technology at scale. Australia has world-class geological storage in its offshore basins.

China:  The world's largest emitter is now the world's fastest-growing CCUS market. China's CCUS Roadmap targets 200 Mtpa by 2060. Sinopec, CNOOC, and China Energy are all running large-scale CCS pilots at power plants and industrial facilities. China's unique challenge: its coal-dominated energy system makes CCUS for power generation more important than anywhere else.

POLITICS, POLICY & REGULATION

CCUS is the most policy-dependent segment of the energy transition — it has limited economic rationale without either a high carbon price or government incentives. The IRA's 45Q is the most powerful CCUS policy globally.

Key Policy Levers MBA Candidates Must Know

What It Means for CCUS

Section 45Q (IRA) — CCUS PTC

$85/tonne CO₂ geologically stored; $60/tonne for EOR; $180/tonne for DAC. 12-year credit window from first year of injection. Transferable under IRA credit transferability. The anchor incentive for all U.S. CCUS investment.

EPA Class VI Injection Wells

Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI permits required for geological CO₂ storage. EPA has issued <20 Class VI permits nationally; 130+ pending. Permitting takes 2–7 years — primary bottleneck for U.S. CCUS deployment. North Dakota and Wyoming have primacy, enabling faster state permitting.

DOE DAC Hubs ($3.5B)

IIJA funded $3.5B for 4 Regional DAC Hubs demonstrating atmospheric CO₂ removal at scale. Stratos Project Bison (Occidental/1PointFive) and Project Cypress (Heirloom/Battelle) are selected hubs. Supplemented by DOE LPO loans.

EU Emissions Trading System (ETS)

EU carbon price (~€60–80/tonne CO₂ in 2025) creates the primary financial incentive for industrial CCUS in Europe — every tonne captured avoids ETS surrender cost. CBAM (effective 2026) extends carbon pricing to imports, making EU-produced CCUS-abated materials more competitive.

UK CCS Infrastructure Fund

£21.7B government commitment to fund two industrial CCS clusters (HyNet, East Coast). Track-1 projects at FID stage. CCUS is central to UK's Industrial Decarbonization Strategy.

Pipeline Permitting (States)

Midwest CO₂ pipeline networks face state-level permitting challenges: Iowa, South Dakota, and North Dakota have all imposed moratoriums or strict requirements. Summit Carbon Solutions has faced multi-year permitting battles — illustrating that state rights are a major CCUS bottleneck beyond EPA permitting.

Carbon Contracts for Difference (EU)

EU Innovation Fund Carbon Contracts for Difference provide guaranteed prices for captured and stored CO₂ — similar to CfD for renewables. Pilot auctions conducted 2024. Designed to bridge the cost gap between CCS economics and EU ETS price.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: DAC Cost Reduction: The Race to $100/tonne  DAC Cost Reduction: The Race to $100/tonne

Direct Air Capture currently costs $400–600/tonne CO₂ at Occidental's Stratos plant. The DOE's target is $100/tonne by 2032 through the DAC Shot program — an aggressive learning curve investment analogous to the Solar Shot ($0.03/kWh for solar). Each doubling of DAC capacity is estimated to reduce cost by 15–20%. At $100/tonne, DAC becomes cost-competitive with many other emissions abatement options and economically viable without subsidies in high-carbon-price markets. The question is how many doublings are needed — and who provides the capital during the valley between current costs and the target.

Trend 2: The CO₂ Transport Infrastructure Buildout  The CO₂ Transport Infrastructure Buildout

The Midwest and Gulf Coast are developing the world's largest CO₂ transport networks. ExxonMobil's Denbury acquisition (1,300 miles of existing CO₂ pipeline) is the foundation. Summit Carbon Solutions (2,000+ mile network connecting Midwest ethanol plants), Navigator CO₂ Ventures, and Wolf Carbon Solutions are building the backbone of a CO₂ logistics infrastructure analogous to the natural gas pipeline system. This infrastructure will be essential for the cost-effective deployment of point-source CCS — without trunk pipelines, every industrial emitter must finance its own dedicated storage, which is prohibitively expensive. The pipeline permitting battles in Iowa and South Dakota are the most immediate constraint.

Trend 3: Industrial Clusters: The Hub Model  Industrial Clusters: The Hub Model

Industrial decarbonization hubs — concentrating multiple emitters (cement, steel, chemicals, refineries) in close geographic proximity to shared CO₂ transport and storage infrastructure — dramatically reduce the per-tonne cost of CCUS by sharing fixed infrastructure costs. The UK's HyNet and East Coast Cluster, Rotterdam's Porthos and Aramis projects, and the U.S. Gulf Coast are the most advanced hub models. The hub model changes the economics fundamentally: instead of each emitter building its own $500M+ pipeline-to-storage link, they pay a toll on shared infrastructure. The infrastructure operator earns a regulated return — an attractive infrastructure investment structure.

Trend 4: Novel Storage: Basalt Mineralization  Novel Storage: Basalt Mineralization

Traditional CO₂ storage in saline aquifers requires decades for CO₂ to mineralize into solid carbonates — the most permanent form of storage. Iceland's Carbfix technology (partnered with Climeworks) demonstrates that dissolving CO₂ in water before injection into basalt rock achieves near-complete mineralization within 2 years, not centuries. This dramatically reduces long-term monitoring requirements and concerns about CO₂ leakage. Carbfix is scaling to other basalt regions (Pacific Northwest, East Africa, Iceland). If replicable at scale, basalt mineralization could become the premium geological storage technology for DAC projects where permanence is paramount.

Trend 5: Carbon Removal Credits: The Premium Market  Carbon Removal Credits: The Premium Market

The voluntary carbon market distinguishes between carbon avoidance credits (avoiding future emissions — lower value) and carbon removal credits (physically removing CO₂ already in the atmosphere — higher value). DAC and BECCS produce carbon removal credits. Microsoft, Stripe, Shopify, and other tech companies are paying $400–1,000/tonne for verified, permanent DAC removal credits to meet Scope 1 residual emissions targets that renewable energy cannot address. Frontier (Stripe-led, with Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Salesforce — $925M committed) is the largest advance market commitment for carbon removal. This corporate demand is the off-take mechanism that makes DAC projects financeable before the $100/tonne cost target is reached.

Trend 6: The EOR Controversy: Oil Company Lifeline or Legitimate Climate Tool?  The EOR Controversy: Oil Company Lifeline or Legitimate Climate Tool?

CO₂-Enhanced Oil Recovery accounts for the majority of current global CCUS capacity — but injects CO₂ into oil reservoirs to produce more oil. The resulting oil, when burned, emits more CO₂ than was stored. Critics argue CO₂-EOR is a fossil fuel subsidy dressed up as climate policy. Proponents argue the CO₂ stays permanently in the reservoir, the 45Q credit incentivizes industrial CO₂ capture that wouldn't otherwise happen, and EOR provides the revenue that makes CCUS projects bankable at current costs. The debate reflects the fundamental tension in CCUS: is it a bridge to a clean future, or a mechanism to perpetuate fossil fuel infrastructure?

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

CCUS offers MBA candidates exposure to one of the most policy-complex and capital-intensive segments of the energy transition — where industrial finance, carbon markets, regulatory strategy, and technology risk intersect.

Project Finance: Project Finance: Large-scale point-source CCS projects are financed as infrastructure with 45Q credits providing revenue certainty (analogous to CfDs for renewables). Typical capital stack: 50–60% debt, 30–40% equity, 45Q credit monetized via tax equity or sale. DOE LPO loan guarantees de-risk first-of-a-kind projects. Key underwriting risks: storage permanence (geological risk), pipeline permitting, and capture technology performance.

CO₂ Transport & Storage Infrastructure: CO₂ Transport & Storage Infrastructure: CO₂ pipeline and storage are natural infrastructure assets — long-lived, regulated, fee-based, with monopoly characteristics in their service areas. The analogy to natural gas pipelines makes them attractive to infrastructure funds (Brookfield, Macquarie, KKR). CO₂ storage could develop as a regulated rate-of-return business similar to natural gas pipeline transport. ExxonMobil's Denbury acquisition was essentially an infrastructure acquisition.

Carbon Markets & Trading: Carbon Markets & Trading: CCUS projects generate 45Q credits (U.S. compliance), EU ETS allowances (avoided), and voluntary carbon removal credits (DAC/BECCS). Carbon trading desks at major banks and commodity firms (Shell, TotalEnergies, Vitol) trade CO₂ allowances. Carbon removal credit brokers (South Pole, Xpansiv, BeZero) develop the voluntary market infrastructure. Understanding carbon market pricing, credit quality standards, and permanence requirements is a differentiating skill.

Venture Capital: Venture Capital: DAC technology companies (Climeworks, Heirloom, 1PointFive, Sustaera), novel storage (Carbfix, Verdox), CO₂ utilization (CarbonCure, Prometheus Fuels, Air Company), and MRV technology (GHGSat, Carbon Mapper) are all active VC categories. Breakthrough Energy Ventures, Prelude Capital, and Congruent Ventures are the most active. The $925M Frontier advance purchase commitment provides a de-risked revenue pathway for early-stage DAC companies.

Key financial concepts: 45Q credit structure (geologic vs. EOR vs. DAC tiers), carbon price dynamics (EU ETS, California CRT, voluntary markets), CCUS project levelized cost (LCOC), Class VI injection well permitting, geological storage capacity and risk, MRV standards (GHG Protocol), and premium removal credit pricing ($400–1,000/tonne for permanent DAC removal).

FUTURE OUTLOOK

CCUS is at a genuine inflection point — the policy support (IRA 45Q, EU ETS, UK CCS Infrastructure Fund) has never been stronger, the technology is proven at point sources, and DAC is beginning its cost learning curve. The challenge is scaling from 50 Mtpa today to the 7,600 Mtpa the IEA requires by 2050 — a 150-fold increase in 25 years. That scale of expansion has never been achieved for any industrial technology. But neither has the financial incentive package that now exists.

Base Case: Base Case: U.S. CCUS capacity grows from ~50 Mtpa to 200+ Mtpa by 2030 as Gulf Coast hubs and Midwest networks develop under 45Q. DAC reaches 5 Mtpa globally by 2030 at costs falling toward $200/tonne. Northern Lights expands; EU industrial clusters advance. Global CCUS market reaches $22–30B by 2034.

Bull Case: Bull Case: EPA Class VI permitting reform accelerates (18-month target); Midwest CO₂ pipeline permitting resolved; DAC cost falls to $150/tonne by 2030; corporate carbon removal demand exceeds supply; China deploys 100+ Mtpa of industrial CCS. Global CCUS reaches 500+ Mtpa by 2035.

Bear Case: Bear Case: Pipeline permitting battles stall Midwest networks; Class VI permits remain backlogged; DAC costs disappoint (remain above $300/tonne); OBBBA reduces 45Q certainty; voluntary carbon markets face credibility crisis. CCUS growth limited to 100–150 Mtpa globally by 2030.

The one certainty: the IEA's Net Zero by 2050 pathway cannot be achieved without CCUS at scale. Hard-to-abate sectors — cement, steel, chemicals, aviation — have no practical decarbonization pathway other than CCUS or hydrogen. The question is not whether CCUS scales, but how quickly costs fall, how effectively policy bridges the gap, and whether the geological storage and transport infrastructure can be built fast enough.

INTERVIEW CONVERSATION STARTERS

"The IRA's $180/tonne 45Q credit for Direct Air Capture is the highest incentive in U.S. clean energy policy — higher than the solar ITC, higher than the wind PTC, higher than the nuclear PTC. It's essentially saying that removing a tonne of CO₂ from the air is worth $180 in tax credits. At Occidental's current cost of $400–600/tonne, the credit covers 30–45% of the cost. The bet is that costs follow the solar learning curve — and at $100/tonne, the math works without subsidies."

"The CCUS industry's fundamental problem is that it needs regulated infrastructure — CO₂ pipelines and storage — that doesn't exist yet, and no single project can justify building it. It's the same chicken-and-egg problem that stalled offshore LNG in the 1960s until Qatar and Australia built the first terminals. ExxonMobil's $4.9 billion Denbury acquisition is the industry's answer — use existing CO₂ pipeline infrastructure as the foundation for a Gulf Coast hub model."

"The Northern Lights project in Norway is the most important CCUS milestone since Sleipner — it's the world's first cross-border CO₂ shipping and storage service, which means that for the first time, a European cement or steel company doesn't need to be sitting on a geological storage formation to do CCS. They can capture their CO₂ and ship it to Norway for storage. That changes the economics for every industrial emitter in northwestern Europe."

"The EOR debate is the most intellectually honest challenge in CCUS. Critics are right that CO₂-EOR produces more oil, which produces more emissions when burned. But proponents are also right that without EOR revenue, most of the U.S. CO₂ pipeline infrastructure would not exist, and many capture projects would not be economically viable. EOR is an uncomfortable but necessary bridge — the infrastructure it finances is real and will outlast the oil."
