INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Hydrogen is simultaneously the most abundant element in the universe and the most geopolitically contested fuel in the energy transition. For MBA candidates, hydrogen sits at the intersection of industrial decarbonization, clean energy policy, and commodity trading in ways that no other technology does — it is the only molecule that can decarbonize steel, cement, aviation, and long-distance shipping where electrification is impractical. Understanding hydrogen means understanding why the IRA's $3/kg production tax credit triggered the largest wave of clean energy investment announcements in U.S. history, why most of those announcements have not translated into operating projects, and why the gap between hydrogen's promise and its commercial reality is the defining challenge in decarbonization finance.

HISTORY & ORIGINS

Hydrogen is not a new energy carrier — it has been produced and used industrially for over a century. The Haber-Bosch process (1909), which uses hydrogen to synthesize ammonia for fertilizers, is arguably the most consequential chemical process in human history — enabling the food production that supports half the world's population. The history of hydrogen as an energy carrier specifically is marked by recurring cycles of enthusiasm and disappointment, driven by the same fundamental challenge: hydrogen is the most energy-dense molecule by weight but the least dense by volume, and storing and transporting it efficiently remains enormously expensive.

1900s–1970s: Industrial Foundation.  Steam methane reforming (SMR) — reacting natural gas with steam to produce hydrogen and CO₂ — became the dominant production process in the 20th century and remains so today. Hydrogen is also co-produced from petroleum refining and used on-site. The Apollo program used liquid hydrogen (LH₂) as rocket fuel, demonstrating the technology's energy density at the cost of extreme engineering complexity. The 1973 oil crisis produced the first wave of "hydrogen economy" enthusiasm — hydrogen as a replacement for fossil fuels — which faded as oil prices normalized.

1990s–2010: Fuel Cells and the First H₂ Economy Wave.  The commercialization of proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cells for automotive applications — pioneered by Ballard Power Systems — triggered a massive wave of investment in hydrogen vehicles. California's ZEV mandate included hydrogen. BMW, Honda, Toyota, and GM all had hydrogen vehicle programs. But costs remained prohibitive, and the "chicken and egg" problem (no refueling infrastructure without vehicles; no vehicles without infrastructure) proved intractable. The hydrogen fuel cell vehicle wave of the 2000s largely collapsed, with only Toyota's Mirai surviving as a niche product.

2015–2021: The Color Spectrum and Policy Momentum.  A new vocabulary emerged for distinguishing hydrogen by production method: grey (SMR, no CCS), blue (SMR + CCS), green (electrolysis + renewables), pink (nuclear electrolysis), turquoise (methane pyrolysis). The EU launched its Hydrogen Strategy (2020), targeting 10 million tonnes of green hydrogen by 2030 and 40 GW of electrolyzers. Australia, Chile, Saudi Arabia, and Japan announced hydrogen export ambitions. The cost of renewable energy fell sufficiently that green hydrogen's long-term economics became plausible for the first time.

2022–2026: IRA Inflection and the Valley of Death.  The IRA's Section 45V hydrogen PTC (up to $3/kg) was the most transformational hydrogen policy in history — it made green hydrogen potentially profitable without relying on voluntary carbon markets. Hundreds of GW of electrolyzer projects were announced. But: over 140 GW of projects in development globally, fewer than 5 GW are operational. The "announcement-to-execution" gap reflects the reality that electrolyzers still cost $4–8/kg to produce green hydrogen while grey is $1.5–2/kg. The IRA PTC bridges the gap — but only for projects that can meet its strict additionality, deliverability, and temporal matching requirements, which have proven difficult in practice. Plug Power's near-bankruptcy (2024) and multiple project deferrals illustrate the challenge.

MARKET STRUCTURE & VALUE CHAIN

The hydrogen value chain is longer and more complex than most energy commodities because hydrogen is not naturally occurring in pure form — it must be produced from another energy source and then transported or converted for end use. Each step adds cost, which is why green hydrogen's economics are so challenging at current scale.

The Hydrogen Value Chain

1. Primary Energy Input

Renewables (solar, wind, hydro, geothermal) for green H₂; natural gas for grey/blue H₂; nuclear for pink H₂. Cost and carbon intensity of the power input determines the type and economics of the hydrogen produced.

2. Electrolysis (for Green H₂)

Water electrolysis splits H₂O into H₂ and O₂ using electricity. Three main technologies: Alkaline (mature, lowest CAPEX, 49% share); PEM (faster response, higher purity, better for variable renewables, 38% share); SOEC (highest efficiency, still maturing). Electrolyzer CAPEX: $500–1,500/kW in 2025; target $200–400/kW by 2030.

3. Steam Methane Reforming (for Grey/Blue H₂)

Reacts natural gas with steam (CH₄ + H₂O → H₂ + CO₂) at high temperature. Cheapest H₂ production method at $1.5–2/kg. "Blue" hydrogen adds CCS to capture 85–95% of CO₂. Remains dominant (96% of supply) in 2025.

4. H₂ Compression & Purification

Hydrogen must be compressed (350–700 bar for vehicles; 30–200 bar for pipelines) or liquefied (-253°C for LH₂ transport). Compression and purification add $1–3/kg to delivered cost. PSA (Pressure Swing Adsorption) for purification.

5. Storage

Compressed gas (high-pressure tanks), liquid hydrogen, metal hydrides, or chemical carriers (ammonia, LOHC — Liquid Organic Hydrogen Carriers). Ammonia (NH₃) is the most practical long-distance H₂ carrier — existing global shipping infrastructure, higher energy density than compressed H₂.

6. Transportation

Pipeline (repurposed gas pipelines or new dedicated H₂ pipelines), tube trailers (short distance), LH₂ tanker ships (Japan-Australia pilot), ammonia tankers. Pipeline transport is cheapest but requires significant infrastructure investment. H₂ embrittlement damages conventional pipelines.

7. End Use

Refining (hydrotreating, hydrocracking): largest current market. Ammonia synthesis (fertilizers, shipping fuel): second largest. Steel (direct reduction of iron — DRI); cement; chemicals. Future: fuel cells for transport, hydrogen turbines for power, sustainable aviation fuel (SAF).

Three application segments drive hydrogen demand:

Industrial Decarbonization (Current + Near-Term): Steel production via hydrogen-based Direct Reduction of Iron (H₂-DRI) is the most commercially advanced green hydrogen application — SSAB's HYBRIT and H2 Green Steel in Sweden, Thyssenkrupp's tkH2Steel in Germany. Ammonia for fertilizers and shipping fuel (green ammonia). Blue hydrogen for refinery applications transitioning from grey.

Transport (Medium-Term): Heavy-duty fuel cell trucks (Nikola, Hyzon, Hyundai XCIENT) for long-haul logistics where batteries are weight-prohibitive. Maritime shipping (ammonia-fueled vessels; Maersk, NYK Line). Aviation (hydrogen-powered aircraft — Airbus ZEROe targets 2035). Hydrogen fueling stations (Japan, South Korea, Germany lead).

Power Generation & Seasonal Storage (Long-Term): Hydrogen turbines (Siemens, Mitsubishi) burning H₂/NH₃ blends for dispatchable power. Underground hydrogen storage in salt caverns for seasonal energy storage. Grid balancing using electrolyzers as controllable loads.

Financial Sponsors & Government Capital

Hydrogen project finance is in its infancy — most green hydrogen projects rely heavily on government support to bridge the cost gap. The U.S. DOE provided $8B for 7 Regional Clean Hydrogen Hubs (H2Hubs), selected in 2023 and in various stages of development. DOE LPO has loan authority for clean hydrogen. The EU Innovation Fund and Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCfDs) support European projects. Private equity (BlackRock, Brookfield, infrastructure funds) is cautiously entering as costs fall. The challenge: green hydrogen's cost structure does not yet support conventional project finance without concessional capital or guaranteed off-take at above-market prices.

ANATOMY OF A GREEN HYDROGEN PRODUCTION FACILITY

A green hydrogen production facility converts electricity (from renewables) and water into hydrogen gas through electrolysis. Understanding its components is essential for evaluating technology risk, capital cost, and operational economics.

Component

Key Specs

Strategic Significance

Renewable Power Source

Solar PV, wind (onshore or offshore), or hybrid; 24/7 power = higher electrolyzer utilization

The #1 cost driver. Green hydrogen economics are dominated by the cost of electricity (~70% of LCOH). Low-cost renewable power ($20–30/MWh) is essential. Co-location with dedicated renewables avoids grid charges.

Electrolyzer Stack

PEM: proton exchange membrane; Alkaline: KOH solution; SOEC: solid oxide ceramic. 1–100+ MW per system

The core conversion technology. Stack lifetime: 80,000–100,000 hours. Degradation rate: 0.5–2%/year. Stack replacement every 7–12 years adds to OPEX. Efficiency: 50–70 kWh/kg H₂.

Power Electronics (Rectifier)

AC-DC conversion; 99%+ efficiency target

Converts AC grid or renewable power to DC for electrolysis. Rectifier quality affects energy efficiency. Major cost item at large scale.

Water Treatment & Deionization

18 MΩ·cm deionized water required for PEM; 9 litres water per kg H₂ produced

Water quality is critical — impurities degrade membranes rapidly. Deionized water system adds CAPEX and OPEX. Water availability is a site selection consideration in arid regions.

H₂ Compression & Drying

30–700 bar compression; PSA or TSA drying to <-60°C dew point

Compression accounts for 10–20% of total energy consumption. High-pressure systems more expensive but enable easier storage and transport.

H₂ Storage

Compressed tanks (350–700 bar), underground caverns, liquid H₂ (-253°C), or ammonia conversion

Storage choice determines project economics. Underground salt caverns (cheapest, large-scale) require specific geology. Liquid H₂ loses 30–40% of energy in liquefaction. Ammonia conversion enables global shipping.

Safety Systems

H₂ leak detection, explosion-proof enclosures, ventilation, emergency shutdown

Hydrogen's wide flammability range (4–75% in air) and low ignition energy require stringent safety design. No industrial fatalities from commercial hydrogen facilities in modern era, but public perception risk remains.

Control & SCADA Systems

AI-driven load following; electrolyzer dispatch optimization

Optimizing electrolyzer operation relative to renewable power availability and hydrogen price signals is critical for economics. Advanced control systems can improve utilization by 15–20%.

How it works: Electricity from renewable sources powers the electrolyzer stack, where water molecules (H₂O) are split at the membrane into hydrogen ions (H⁺) that migrate across the membrane and recombine as H₂ gas, and oxygen (O₂) released at the anode. The H₂ is compressed, dried, and either stored on-site or transported to end users. The key efficiency metric is kilowatt-hours per kilogram of hydrogen produced (kWh/kg H₂) — lower is better. Current systems: 50–70 kWh/kg; theoretical minimum: 39 kWh/kg. The cost of electricity dominates total hydrogen cost — at $30/MWh electricity and 55 kWh/kg efficiency, electricity alone costs $1.65/kg, before CAPEX, OPEX, or water.

GLOBAL GEOGRAPHY & COUNTRY LEADERS

Europe:  The most advanced policy and project development ecosystem. The EU Hydrogen Strategy targets 40 GW of European electrolyzer capacity by 2030 and 10 million tonnes of green hydrogen production — both targets now widely considered unreachable at current pace. Germany (NortH2, RWE projects), Netherlands (Rotterdam as Europe's green H₂ import hub, HyTransPort), Norway (Equinor, Yara ammonia), and Spain/Portugal (natural hydrogen export potential) lead. The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) will make EU-produced green hydrogen more competitive vs. imported grey alternatives.

United States:  The IRA's 45V PTC (up to $3/kg for 10 years) is the most powerful green hydrogen incentive globally. Seven Regional Clean Hydrogen Hubs (H2Hubs) are in development across the country — Pacific Northwest (Intermountain Power), Gulf Coast (HyVelocity), Appalachian (ARCH₂), Mid-Atlantic (MACH2), and others. The strict additionality and temporal matching requirements of 45V (new renewable power, delivered in the same hour as hydrogen production, in the same grid region) have slowed project development — but projects meeting these requirements access the most generous incentive available anywhere.

Middle East (Saudi Arabia, UAE):  Neom's Green Hydrogen project ($8.4B, Air Products/ACWA Power JV) is the world's largest green H₂ project under development — 4 GW of electrolysis, powered by 4 GW of solar and wind, producing 600 tonnes/day of green ammonia for export by 2026. Saudi Arabia's SABIC and Saudi Aramco are developing blue hydrogen infrastructure. The UAE (ADNOC) is combining blue H₂ from gas with clean ammonia for export.

Australia:  Positioned as a major green hydrogen exporter to Japan, Korea, and Europe — leveraging cheap solar and wind, available land, and deep port infrastructure. The National Hydrogen Strategy targets 15% of global export markets by 2030. Fortescue Future Industries (Andrew Forrest), Origin Energy, and bp Australia are the leading project developers. The Australia-Japan Green Hydrogen Supply Chain pilot demonstrated LH₂ transport for the first time.

Japan & South Korea:  The two most advanced hydrogen import markets. Japan's Hydrogen Society roadmap targets 3 million tonnes of hydrogen by 2030 and 20 million tonnes by 2050. JERA, ENEOS, and Kawasaki Heavy Industries are building LH₂ import terminals. South Korea's Hydrogen Economy Roadmap targets 5 million fuel cell vehicles by 2040. Both countries are the primary drivers of global LH₂ shipping and ammonia-as-hydrogen-carrier infrastructure development.

POLITICS, POLICY & REGULATION

Hydrogen is the most policy-dependent energy technology — without government support, green hydrogen cannot compete with grey on cost in most markets. The policy landscape is simultaneously the most promising and most complex in clean energy.

Key Policy Levers MBA Candidates Must Know

Policy / Lever

What It Means for Hydrogen

Section 45V (IRA) — Clean H₂ PTC

Up to $3/kg production tax credit for 10 years for clean hydrogen meeting lifecycle emissions thresholds. Four tiers based on carbon intensity: $3/kg at <0.45 kg CO₂eq/kg H₂ (essentially green H₂ only). Survived OBBBA with phase-out beginning 2033. The most powerful global hydrogen incentive.

45V Additionality Rules (Treasury)

To claim $3/kg, hydrogen must be produced from: (1) NEW renewable energy (additionality), (2) temporally matched (same hour, from 2028), (3) geographically matched (same grid region). These rules eliminated most projects from qualifying — triggering policy debate about whether rules are too strict.

DOE H2Hubs ($8B)

7 Regional Clean Hydrogen Hubs funded under IIJA. Pacific Northwest, Gulf Coast, Mid-Atlantic, Appalachian, Midwest, California, and Mountain clusters. Goal: demonstrate green/blue H₂ at scale. Construction underway as of 2025.

EU Hydrogen Strategy

Targets 10 Mt green H₂ by 2030 in Europe (5 Mt domestic + 5 Mt imported). EU Delegated Acts define "renewable fuel of non-biological origin" (RFNBO) for regulatory purposes — equivalent to 45V additionality for European markets. CBAM will price imported grey H₂.

EU Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCfDs)

Guaranteed price for green hydrogen or green steel, compensating for the higher cost vs. grey alternatives. Germany's H₂Global and Netherlands' SDE++ are the primary CCfD mechanisms. Designed to bridge the "green premium" until hydrogen costs fall.

Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) & Clean Fuel Standards

U.S. RFS and California LCFS both allow green hydrogen as a compliance pathway, creating additional revenue streams for hydrogen producers serving transportation markets.

Japanese/Korean Hydrogen Import Subsidies

Japan and South Korea are subsidizing long-term hydrogen import contracts to develop global supply chains. JOGMEC (Japan) and KOGAS (Korea) are providing concessional financing for overseas green H₂ projects that commit to export.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: The Electrolyzer Cost Curve  The Electrolyzer Cost Curve

Electrolyzer CAPEX has fallen from ~$2,000/kW (2015) to ~$500–1,500/kW (2025) and is projected to reach $200–400/kW by 2030. Chinese alkaline electrolyzers are already below $250/kW at utility scale. The cost trajectory closely mirrors the solar panel learning curve — each doubling of cumulative deployed capacity reduces cost by 15–20%. The critical question for green hydrogen's commercial viability is whether Western electrolyzer manufacturers (Nel, ThyssenKrupp, Siemens) can close the cost gap with Chinese manufacturers, or whether FEOC-equivalent restrictions on Chinese electrolyzers will prevent the lowest-cost technology from being deployed in the U.S. and EU.

Trend 2: Blue Hydrogen as the Bridge  Blue Hydrogen as the Bridge

Grey hydrogen (SMR + no CCS) is cheap ($1.5–2/kg) but carbon-intensive. Green hydrogen is clean but expensive ($4–8/kg). Blue hydrogen (SMR + CCS) sits in between ($2–4/kg, 85–95% lower carbon than grey). For hard-to-abate sectors that need hydrogen now — refineries, ammonia plants, some industrial applications — blue hydrogen is the practical near-term solution. Equinor's H2H Saltend, bp's H2Teesside, and ExxonMobil's Baytown projects represent the U.S. and European blue hydrogen pipeline. The IRA's 45V credit also applies to blue hydrogen meeting lifecycle thresholds — creating financial incentives for CCS-integrated hydrogen production.

Trend 3: Ammonia as the Global H₂ Carrier  Ammonia as the Global H₂ Carrier

Pure hydrogen is extremely difficult to transport over long distances — liquefaction at -253°C loses 30–40% of energy, and high-pressure compression is expensive. Green ammonia (NH₃) — produced by combining green H₂ with nitrogen — is the most practical solution. Ammonia has 50% higher energy density than liquid H₂, uses existing global shipping infrastructure (600+ ammonia tankers), and can either be used directly as a fuel (maritime shipping, power generation) or "cracked" back to hydrogen at the destination. Japan's Hydrogen Council and Australia's ARENA are actively developing ammonia import-export supply chains. Maersk's methanol dual-fuel ships and the IMO's carbon intensity regulations are driving green ammonia as a maritime fuel.

Trend 4: Hard-to-Abate Sectors: Steel’s H₂ Moment  Hard-to-Abate Sectors: Steel's H₂ Moment

Steel production accounts for ~7% of global CO₂ emissions. The dominant steelmaking process — blast furnace basic oxygen furnace (BF-BOF) — uses coal (coke) as both a reducing agent and energy source. Hydrogen Direct Reduction of Iron (H₂-DRI) replaces coal with hydrogen, producing steam instead of CO₂. H2 Green Steel (Sweden), SSAB HYBRIT, and ThyssenKrupp's tkH2Steel are the commercial-scale pioneers. ArcelorMittal and Nucor are evaluating H₂-DRI for new capacity. The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM, fully effective 2026) is pricing green steel's carbon advantage — a potential $50–100/tonne revenue uplift that changes H₂-DRI economics fundamentally.

Trend 5: The 45V Additionality Debate  The 45V Additionality Debate

The Treasury's 45V rules — requiring new renewable power, delivered in the same hour, in the same grid region — are simultaneously the most rigorous and most controversial hydrogen policy in the world. Advocates say additionality prevents green hydrogen from simply redirecting existing renewables and claiming credit for emissions reductions that never happened. Critics say the rules are so strict that they kill virtually every project — Plug Power, Constellation, and other developers have argued that the hourly matching requirement makes projects unbankable. The DOE's $8B H2Hubs are proceeding despite the policy ambiguity, but private capital is waiting for regulatory clarity.

Trend 6: Methane Pyrolysis: Turquoise Hydrogen  Methane Pyrolysis: Turquoise Hydrogen

An emerging alternative to both SMR and electrolysis: methane pyrolysis (also called "turquoise" hydrogen) splits natural gas into hydrogen and solid carbon (carbon black) — a potentially valuable co-product used in tires, pigments, and electronics. The process requires heat (from renewable electricity or the hydrogen itself) but no water, and produces solid carbon that can be sequestered or sold rather than CO₂ emissions requiring geological storage. Molten Industries (backed by Breakthrough Energy Ventures, presented at Columbia by one of the primer's authors) and C-Zero are the leading U.S. startups. If carbon black markets absorb the co-product, turquoise hydrogen could achieve near-negative net carbon intensity at competitive costs.

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

Hydrogen offers MBA candidates exposure to one of the most complex and policy-dependent investment landscapes in clean energy — where government support, technology risk, and market development intersect.

Project Finance: Project Finance: Green hydrogen projects are not yet conventionally bankable at commercial scale without concessional capital. The standard structure: DOE loan guarantee or grant (covering 30–50% of capital), equity from developer and strategic partner, and debt only once offtake is secured at a price that covers debt service. Key challenge: hydrogen lacks a liquid spot market — every project requires a bilateral offtake agreement, which is rare and priced at a premium.

Venture Capital & Growth Equity: Venture Capital & Growth Equity: The most active investment category in hydrogen. Electrolyzer companies (Nel, ITM, Plug Power — public), breakthrough technologies (Molten Industries, C-Zero for methane pyrolysis), hydrogen safety and monitoring (Sensirion, H2Scan), green ammonia (Fortescue, ACWA Power), and H₂ infrastructure (pipeline, fueling) are all active VC categories. Breakthrough Energy Ventures, Prelude Capital, and Energy Impact Partners are the most active climate-focused VCs.

Industrial Corporate Strategy: Industrial Corporate Strategy: Every steel company, chemical company, and refiner has a hydrogen decarbonization strategy. ThyssenKrupp, ArcelorMittal, Basell, BASF, and the major oil companies (bp, Shell, TotalEnergies) are all transitioning their internal hydrogen supply from grey to blue to green. Corporate sustainability roles at these companies now require deep hydrogen economics literacy.

Government & Development Finance: Government & Development Finance: DOE H2Hubs, DOE LPO, DFC, and equivalent bodies in the EU (EIB, Innovation Fund), Australia (ARENA), and Japan (JOGMEC) are the primary capital providers for first-of-a-kind hydrogen infrastructure. Understanding how to structure concessional financing for nascent energy technologies is a differentiating skill in energy and climate finance.

Key financial concepts: Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH), electricity cost sensitivity (70% of LCOH), electrolyzer utilization rate (higher = lower LCOH), stack lifetime and replacement cost, 45V credit structure and additionality requirements, hydrogen vs. ammonia as an energy carrier, and the green premium required for industrial offtake.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

Hydrogen's transition from industrial feedstock to clean energy carrier will be the defining decarbonization challenge of the 2030s. The technology exists. The policy support (IRA, EU Hydrogen Strategy) exists. The demand — from steel, ammonia, shipping, and eventually power — is real. The gap is economics: green hydrogen must fall from $4–8/kg to below $2/kg to compete with grey, and that requires the same kind of learning-curve investment that drove solar from $0.50/watt to $0.03/watt.

Base Case: Base Case: Green hydrogen costs reach $2–3/kg in best-resource locations (Middle East, Chile, Australia) by 2030. U.S. and European costs reach $3–5/kg. IRA 45V and EU CCfDs bridge the remaining gap for industrial applications. Green steel, green ammonia, and blue hydrogen for refining are the primary commercial-scale markets. Electrolyzer market reaches $30B+ by 2030.

Bull Case: Bull Case: Electrolyzer costs fall to $200/kW (Chinese benchmark achieved globally); 45V additionality rules relaxed; green hydrogen reaches $1.5/kg in best locations by 2032; green steel becomes cost-competitive in EU due to CBAM; maritime shipping mandates accelerate green ammonia demand. Global green H₂ market exceeds $100B by 2030.

Bear Case: Bear Case: 45V rules remain strict and kill most U.S. projects; OBBBA reduces H2Hub funding; electrolyzer learning curve disappoints; grey H₂ remains too cheap for green to compete without subsidy; industrial offtakers unwilling to pay green premium without regulatory mandates. Green H₂ remains sub-1% of total hydrogen by 2030.

The one certainty: the IEA's Net Zero by 2050 pathway requires 150 million tonnes of hydrogen annually by 2030 — three times current global production. That scale of expansion cannot happen without hydrogen becoming a major capital market asset class. The window of opportunity for investors and advisors who understand hydrogen economics is now.

"The IRA's Section 45V is the most powerful hydrogen incentive ever enacted — $3/kg for 10 years is genuinely transformational economics. But the Treasury's additionality and hourly matching rules have made it essentially uncountable by most projects. The policy intention was right; the implementation is the problem. The question for any hydrogen project in the U.S. right now is not whether the economics work — it's whether you can structure an asset that actually qualifies for the credit."

"The gap between announced electrolyzer projects and operating ones — 140 GW announced, under 5 GW operating — is the single most important data point in hydrogen. It tells you that hydrogen's challenge is not technology, not policy, and not capital availability in isolation. It's all three simultaneously: technology that isn't cheap enough yet, policy that isn't clear enough, and capital that won't commit without bilateral offtake agreements at prices no one wants to pay."

"H2 Green Steel raising $7 billion for green H₂-based steelmaking is the most important data point for anyone in infrastructure finance. It proves that industrial customers will sign long-term green steel contracts at a premium — which is the only way to make green hydrogen economics work. If Mercedes-Benz and Scania will pay a green premium for steel in their supply chains, the hydrogen economy is not just a government subsidy story — it's a corporate procurement story."

"Ammonia is the Trojan horse for green hydrogen. The countries that figure out ammonia infrastructure first — Japan, South Korea, Netherlands — will control the distribution network for the global green hydrogen trade, the same way LNG terminal operators control natural gas trade today."
