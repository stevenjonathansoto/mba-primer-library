INDUSTRY AT A GLANCE

Electric vehicles are the largest demand driver for critical minerals, the centerpiece of global industrial policy competition, and the defining consumer technology transformation of the 2020s. For MBA candidates, EVs sit at the intersection of automotive strategy, battery technology, geopolitics, supply chain finance, and infrastructure investing. Understanding the EV industry means understanding how China built an unchallengeable manufacturing cost advantage, why BYD overtook Tesla as the world's largest EV maker, and why U.S. policy is simultaneously trying to accelerate EV adoption and block Chinese competition.

HISTORY & ORIGINS

The electric vehicle is not a new invention — the first practical EV was built by Robert Anderson around 1832, and at the turn of the 20th century EVs outsold gasoline cars in the United States. Thomas Edison drove an electric vehicle. Pope Manufacturing was America's largest automobile maker in 1900, and it made electric cars. The internal combustion engine won the 20th century thanks to cheap oil, Charles Kettering's electric starter (eliminating the hand crank), and Henry Ford's assembly line economics. But the underlying technology — electric motors, batteries, regenerative braking — never disappeared.

1990s–2008: The EV1 and the False Start.  General Motors' EV1 (1996–1999) was the first modern mass-produced EV — and its controversial recall and destruction became the subject of the documentary "Who Killed the Electric Car?" California's ZEV mandate forced OEMs to produce EVs; when the mandate was weakened, most complied by eliminating their EV programs. The Toyota Prius (1997) introduced hybrid technology to the mainstream. Tesla Motors was founded in 2003 by Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning, with Elon Musk joining as chairman and leading the 2004 Series A.

2008–2015: Tesla’s Bet and the Subsidy Era.  2008–2015: Tesla's Bet and the Subsidy Era. Tesla's Roadster (2008) proved a compelling electric sports car was possible. The Chevy Volt (2010) and Nissan LEAF (2010) brought EVs to mass market with federal subsidies. The $7,500 federal tax credit (Section 30D, introduced 2008) became the primary demand driver for U.S. EV adoption. Meanwhile, China launched its "New Energy Vehicle" (NEV) policy in 2009, providing massive subsidies to build both EV demand and domestic manufacturing capacity. China's strategic intent was clear: leapfrog ICE technology and build global dominance in the next generation of transportation.

2016–2021: Tesla’s Dominance and China’s Build-Out.  2016–2021: Tesla's Dominance and China's Build-Out. Tesla's Model 3 (2017) was the inflection point — a mass-market BEV that achieved genuine range and performance at a $35,000 price point. Global EV sales surged from 2 million (2019) to 6.6 million (2021). BYD, which had been building buses and commercial vehicles, launched its competitive passenger EV lineup (Han, Tang, Seal). Chinese domestic EV subsidies were massive — estimated $57B from 2009 to 2022 — funding the manufacturing scale-up that would eventually produce the lowest-cost EVs in the world.

2022–2024: The IRA Inflection and China’s Cost Advantage Crystallizes.  2022–2024: The IRA Inflection and China's Cost Advantage Crystallizes. The IRA (August 2022) restructured the U.S. EV tax credit with FEOC restrictions — requiring battery minerals and components not sourced from China to qualify for the $7,500 credit. This was simultaneously the most ambitious U.S. clean vehicle policy ever and a direct industrial policy attack on Chinese supply chains. Meanwhile, BYD's Seagull (priced at ~$10,000 in China) demonstrated that Chinese OEMs had achieved a structural cost advantage that no Western automaker could match. Global EV sales reached 17 million in 2024.

2025–2026: OBBBA Credit Elimination and the “Green Cold War.”  2025–2026: OBBBA Credit Elimination and the "Green Cold War." The OBBBA (July 4, 2025) eliminated the $7,500 consumer EV credit effective September 30, 2025. The impact was immediate: Kia EV6 sales fell 71% month-over-month after the credit expired. Tesla's annual sales fell 8.6% — its worst year ever. BYD outsold Tesla globally for the first time (2.26M vs. 1.64M units), including surpassing Tesla in European monthly registrations in August 2025. The 100% U.S. tariff on Chinese EVs remained intact. The EU's tiered tariffs (7.8% for Tesla, 35.3% for SAIC) created a complex multi-market competitive landscape. Ford CEO Jim Farley publicly reoriented Ford's strategy around matching BYD's cost structure.

MARKET STRUCTURE & VALUE CHAIN

The EV value chain spans from raw mineral extraction through battery cell manufacturing, vehicle assembly, software development, and charging infrastructure. China dominates the middle of the chain — battery cells — with the same structural implications as for solar panels and BESS.

The EV Value Chain (Upstream → Downstream)

1. Critical Minerals

Lithium (Chile, Australia, Argentina), cobalt (DRC), nickel (Indonesia), graphite (China), rare earths (China) for permanent magnet motors. Same FEOC supply chain challenges as BESS. China processes ~70–95% of most critical minerals.

2. Battery Cells

The most strategically important stage. CATL (~37% global share), BYD, CALB, Gotion dominate. Non-China: LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI, Panasonic. Chinese cell costs ~$60–70/kWh vs. $90–100/kWh for non-Chinese producers — a 30–40% cost advantage that flows directly to vehicle price.

3. Battery Packs

Cells assembled into packs with thermal management, BMS, and structural integration. China leads; U.S. gigafactories (Ultium, BlueOval SK, Stellantis StarPlus) building scale. LFP chemistry (BYD Blade battery) winning in mass market; NMC in premium and long-range segments.

4. Electric Motors & Powertrains

Permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSM) dominate; rare earth magnets critical. China dominates motor manufacturing and rare earth processing. Tesla's in-house motor design (carbon fiber rotor) is a differentiator. BMW's in-house motor production reduces exposure.

5. Vehicle Platform & Assembly

Dedicated EV platforms (Tesla Model 3/Y, VW MEB, GM Ultium, BYD e-platform 3.0) outperform adapted ICE platforms in range, performance, and cost efficiency. China leads in vehicle assembly volume; Germany in premium; U.S. in full-size EVs.

6. Software & Operating System

The most underrated differentiator. Tesla's over-the-air (OTA) software update capability is years ahead of legacy OEMs. BYD's DiLink system. China's Huawei-derived automotive OS (AITO). "Software-defined vehicle" is the industry's defining technological challenge for legacy OEMs.

7. Charging Infrastructure

AC Level 2 (home/destination) and DC Fast Charging (public). Tesla Supercharger network is the gold standard; NACS connector now adopted as U.S. standard. ChargePoint, EVgo, Blink, and utilities are building public networks. China has the world's largest and most dense charging network.

Three market segments define EV deployment:

Passenger Cars (89% of market): The dominant segment. Mass-market BEVs (BYD Seagull, Tesla Model 3/Y, VW ID.4, Hyundai IONIQ 6) and premium BEVs (Tesla Model S/X, BMW i7, Mercedes EQS, Porsche Taycan). China leads with >50% EV share of new car sales; Europe ~25%; U.S. ~10%.

Commercial Vehicles & Trucks: Electric buses (BYD, Yutong dominate globally), electric delivery vans (Mercedes eSprinter, Ford E-Transit, Rivian EDV for Amazon), and electric Class 8 trucks (Tesla Semi, Daimler eActros, Volvo FM Electric). Commercial fleet electrification is driven by total cost of ownership (TCO) — lower fuel and maintenance costs offset higher purchase price over 5–10 year fleet cycles.

Two- and Three-Wheelers: The world's most electrified vehicle category — predominantly in Asia. Hero Electric, Ola Electric (India), Gogoro (Taiwan), and hundreds of Chinese manufacturers serve a market of 40M+ electric motorcycles and scooters annually. Often overlooked in Western analysis but critically important for understanding global EV adoption rates.

ANATOMY OF AN ELECTRIC VEHICLE

An electric vehicle replaces the internal combustion engine, fuel tank, transmission, and exhaust system with a battery pack, electric motor(s), power electronics, and a thermal management system. Understanding what changes — and what stays the same — is essential for evaluating OEM strategy, supplier disruption, and manufacturing cost structures.

Component

Key Specs

Strategic Significance

Battery Pack

60–100+ kWh; LFP or NMC chemistry; 400V or 800V architecture

The single most expensive component (~40% of vehicle cost). 800V systems (Hyundai E-GMP, Porsche J1) enable ultra-fast charging (20 min for 200 miles). BYD's Blade LFP eliminates thermal runaway risk with cell-to-pack design.

Battery Management System (BMS)

Cell-level monitoring; SOC, SOH, temperature

Software-driven safety and performance system. Tesla's BMS and thermal management is widely considered the industry gold standard. OTA updates allow continuous improvement after sale.

Electric Motor(s)

PMSM (permanent magnet) or induction; front/rear/AWD configurations

Single or dual motor configurations. Permanent magnet motors require rare earth magnets (China-dominant). Tesla's carbon fiber-wrapped rotor enables higher RPM. Motor efficiency (>95%) vs. ICE (~25–40%) is fundamental EV advantage.

Power Electronics (Inverter/Onboard Charger)

DC-AC inverter; AC onboard charger; DC-DC converter

Converts battery DC to AC for motor; converts grid AC to DC for charging. SiC (silicon carbide) semiconductor technology replacing older IGBT for better efficiency at high voltage.

Thermal Management

Battery cooling + cabin HVAC via heat pump

LFP chemistry less thermally sensitive than NMC; still requires active cooling above ~35°C. Heat pumps (Tesla, Hyundai) dramatically improve winter range vs. resistive heating. Critical for cold-climate performance.

Chassis & Body

Dedicated EV skateboard platform or adapted ICE platform

Skateboard platform (flat battery floor, motors at axles) enables lower center of gravity, better interior space, and optimized weight distribution. Adapted ICE platforms compromise on all dimensions.

Software / Vehicle OS

OTA updates; driver assistance; infotainment; telematics

The defining competitive frontier. Tesla's OTA update capability has been releasing new features for 10+ years post-sale. Legacy OEM software (VW CARIAD, GM Super Cruise) significantly lagged. BYD's DiLink system closing the gap in China.

Charging Port

CCS2 (EU/legacy U.S.), NACS (Tesla, now U.S. standard), CHAdeMO (Japan/declining)

NACS is now the U.S. standard — Ford, GM, Rivian, Volvo, Mercedes all adopted. ChargePoint, EVgo, and Electrify America upgrading to NACS. DC fast charging speed: 50–350 kW depending on vehicle and charger.

How it works: The battery pack stores electricity (typically 60–100 kWh). When the driver accelerates, the inverter converts DC battery power to AC electricity that drives the electric motor(s). The motor converts electrical energy to rotational mechanical energy, driving the wheels directly (no multi-speed transmission needed). Regenerative braking reverses the process — the motor becomes a generator, converting kinetic energy back to electricity and recharging the battery. Net efficiency: ~85–90% well-to-wheel vs. ~15–25% for ICE vehicles.

GLOBAL GEOGRAPHY & COUNTRY LEADERS

China:  The world's dominant EV market and manufacturer. China's EV share of new car sales exceeded 50% in 2025 — the first major automotive market to reach this milestone. BYD's Seagull ($10,000) and Dolphin ($15,000) have demonstrated that Chinese manufacturers have achieved structural cost advantages that are essentially uncatchable in the near term. China's integrated supply chain — from lithium processing to cell manufacturing to vehicle assembly — produces EVs at $60–70/kWh battery cost, versus $90–100/kWh for non-Chinese producers. The 100% U.S. tariff and EU countervailing duties are preventing direct market competition but cannot eliminate the cost advantage.

United States:  EV market share of ~10% in 2025 — significantly below Europe and China. The elimination of the $7,500 tax credit under OBBBA has created a near-term headwind, with several models seeing 50–70% monthly sales drops immediately post-credit expiry. Tesla dominates U.S. EV sales with ~50%+ market share. The Big Three (GM, Ford, Stellantis) have built or are building domestic gigafactories under DOE LPO loans but face difficult profitability questions — Ford's EV division (Model e) was losing ~$5B annually in 2024. The 100% tariff on Chinese EVs blocks the most cost-competitive vehicles from the market.

Europe:  EV market share ~25% in 2025, driven by EU CO₂ fleet targets (mandating zero-emission new car sales by 2035). Germany, Norway (90%+ EV share), UK, and France lead. BYD surpassed Tesla in European monthly registrations in August 2025. EU countervailing duties (tiered, 7.8–35.3%) are partially effective at protecting European OEMs but have not stopped Chinese market penetration. VW, Stellantis, and Renault are under severe profit pressure from Chinese competition and the cost of EV platform transition.

Emerging Markets:  The most surprising EV growth story. Vietnam reached ~40% EV share of new car sales in 2025 (VinFast dominance); Thailand exceeded 20%; Turkey hit 17%. These markets are often served by Chinese OEMs who bypass Western tariffs by building local factories — BYD operates production facilities in Hungary, Thailand, and Brazil. The "EV leapfrog" phenomenon (Ember Energy, Dec 2025) describes emerging markets bypassing the ICE era entirely — driven by Chinese EVs at Chinese prices, without Western tariff protection.

South Korea & Japan:  South Korea (Hyundai/Kia, Samsung SDI, LG Energy Solution) is a global EV and battery leader competing directly with Chinese and American players. Japan's EV transition has been slower — Toyota's hybrid focus left it behind in pure BEV — but Toyota is now accelerating its BEV program and has made significant solid-state battery investments. Honda and Nissan are struggling with EV competitiveness.

POLITICS, POLICY & REGULATION

EV policy is the most contested terrain in clean energy — intersecting consumer choice, national industrial strategy, geopolitical competition with China, and climate commitments. The OBBBA's elimination of consumer EV credits represents the most significant U.S. EV policy reversal since the program began.

Key Policy Levers MBA Candidates Must Know

Policy / Lever

What It Means for EVs

Section 30D Credit (Eliminated)

$7,500 consumer EV tax credit eliminated September 30, 2025 under OBBBA. Also eliminated: $4,000 used EV credit (25E), $7,500 commercial EV credit (45W). Home charging infrastructure (30C) credit ending June 2026. Immediate demand shock — multiple models saw 50–70% monthly sales drops.

FEOC Restrictions (IRA, tightened by OBBBA)

Battery minerals/components from China, Russia, Iran, or North Korea disqualify EVs from tax credits. Escalating thresholds through 2027. Driving restructuring of battery supply chains. Major financial burden for Korean, Japanese, and European OEMs who sourced from China.

100% U.S. Tariff on Chinese EVs

First imposed under Biden Section 301, retained under Trump. Blocks direct retail competition from Chinese OEMs (BYD, Geely, SAIC, NIO) in the U.S. market. Creates a price umbrella for U.S. and allied OEMs. BYD responds by building factories in non-tariff jurisdictions (Hungary, Thailand, Brazil).

EU 2035 Zero-Emission Vehicle Mandate

All new cars and vans sold in the EU must be zero-emission by 2035. The most powerful EV demand driver globally after China's NEV mandate. Forces VW, Stellantis, Renault to accelerate EV transition regardless of profitability. Under political pressure for review/postponement as of 2026.

EU Countervailing Duties on Chinese EVs

Tiered tariffs based on subsidy level: Tesla 7.8%, BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3% (on top of standard 10%). Price undertaking negotiations with China ongoing. Less effective than U.S. 100% tariff at blocking Chinese competition.

DOE Loan Programs Office (LPO)

$40B+ in EV/battery lending authority under IRA. Loans to Ford BlueOval (BlueOval City, TN), Rivian Georgia factory, Ultium Cells (GM/LG JV). Critical capital provider for domestic gigafactory buildout under FEOC compliance requirements.

NACS Connector Standard

North American Charging Standard (Tesla) adopted by Ford, GM, Rivian, Volvo, Mercedes, and all major charging networks in 2023–2024. Creates unified U.S. charging ecosystem — eliminates the "format war" that slowed adoption. CCS2 remains the EU standard.

INDUSTRY TRENDS & TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION

Trend 1: BYD's Cost Structure as the Industry Benchmark  BYD's Cost Structure as the Industry Benchmark

Ford CEO Jim Farley's public statement that BYD is "best in class on cost, supply chain, manufacturing, and intellectual property" — and that Ford is now explicitly benchmarking its strategy against BYD rather than Tesla — is the most significant strategic admission in the auto industry since GM's 2009 bankruptcy. BYD's Seagull retails at ~$10,000 in China; the U.S. average new car price is $51,456. The 100% tariff prevents direct competition, but the structural cost advantage cannot be tariffed away — it reflects Chinese cell manufacturing costs, labor rates, supply chain integration, and manufacturing process innovation that will persist regardless of trade policy.

Trend 2: Software-Defined Vehicles and the OTA Imperative  Software-Defined Vehicles and the OTA Imperative

Tesla's over-the-air software update capability — which has been improving vehicle performance, range, safety, and features for 10+ years post-sale — is the EV industry's most underappreciated competitive differentiator. Legacy OEMs are spending billions to catch up: VW's CARIAD software unit has been a catastrophic failure (billions spent, years of delay); GM's Ultifi and Ford's development of a unified vehicle OS are works in progress. The software-defined vehicle thesis holds that future vehicle margins will come from software subscriptions, autonomous driving fees, and OTA upgrade sales — not just hardware. China's Huawei-derived automotive OS (AITO, used by Seres) is outcompeting legacy OEM software in the Chinese market.

Trend 3: LFP Chemistry Goes Global  LFP Chemistry Goes Global

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries — the chemistry favored by BYD and Chinese BESS manufacturers — are displacing NMC in the mass-market EV segment globally. LFP offers superior thermal safety (no thermal runaway), longer cycle life (3,000–6,000+ cycles), and significantly lower cobalt content — at the cost of ~30% lower energy density than NMC. Tesla introduced LFP in its Standard Range models in 2021; VW followed. LFP's global adoption is reshaping critical mineral demand — reducing cobalt requirements while increasing lithium and graphite needs — and further concentrating supply chains in China, where essentially all LFP cell production occurs.

Trend 4: Autonomous Driving as the EV Endgame  Autonomous Driving as the EV Endgame

The ultimate value of an electric vehicle platform — from an investor perspective — lies in its potential for autonomous operation. Tesla's pivot toward Robotaxi (Cybercab announced 2024, Austin launch planned 2025) and humanoid robots (Optimus) reflects the thesis that the most valuable future business is not selling vehicles but providing autonomous transportation as a service. Waymo is operating commercial robotaxi service in San Francisco, Phoenix, and Austin. The convergence of EVs, autonomous driving, and AI hardware represents the largest transformation of urban mobility in a century — and the largest potential value creation event in automotive history.

Trend 5: Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) and the EV as a Grid Asset  Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) and the EV as a Grid Asset

As EV adoption scales, the aggregate battery capacity of vehicle fleets becomes comparable to grid-scale BESS installations. Vehicle-to-grid technology allows EVs to export power back to the grid during peak demand periods, earning revenue for owners and providing grid services. Ford's F-150 Lightning offers V2G capability (15 kWh/day export). If even 10% of U.S. EVs export 5 kWh daily during peak periods, that represents 500 GWh/day of grid support — more than all installed U.S. BESS capacity in 2023. Utilities and grid operators are developing V2G tariff structures; V2G could fundamentally change the economics of EV ownership.

Trend 6: The “Local-for-Local” Manufacturing Mandate  The "Local-for-Local" Manufacturing Mandate

The combination of FEOC restrictions, tariffs, and national industrial policy is driving a "local-for-local" manufacturing model — where vehicles must be produced in the market where they are sold using locally sourced components to qualify for incentives. BYD is building factories in Hungary (for the EU), Thailand (for Southeast Asia), and Brazil (for Latin America) to bypass tariffs. Hyundai built its Metaplant in Georgia; Ford has BlueOval City in Tennessee. The consequence is higher global manufacturing costs (less scale-efficient than centralized Chinese production), but reduced geopolitical risk and domestic job creation. This de-globalization trend will persist regardless of who controls the White House.

INVESTMENT, FINANCE & CAREER ANGLES

The EV industry intersects with multiple finance functions — from public equities and debt markets for large OEMs, to infrastructure finance for gigafactories and charging networks, to venture capital for autonomous driving and next-generation battery technology.

Public Equities: Public Equities: Tesla (NASDAQ: TSLA) is the defining pure-play EV stock — trading on the AI/robotics thesis as much as auto fundamentals. BYD (HKEX: 1211) is the Chinese counterpart. Rivian (RIVN) and Lucid (LCID) are the EV startup pure-plays, both burning significant cash. Battery pure-plays: LG Energy Solution (KRX), Samsung SDI (KRX), Panasonic (TYO), CATL (SZE). Charging networks: ChargePoint (CHPT), EVgo (EVGO). Semiconductor exposure: ON Semiconductor, STMicroelectronics (SiC for power electronics).

Infrastructure Finance (Gigafactories): Infrastructure Finance (Gigafactories): EV battery gigafactories are financed as large industrial projects — DOE LPO loans (Ford BlueOval: $9.2B, Rivian: $6.6B), state incentive packages, and OEM balance sheet capital. FEOC compliance is now a financing condition — lenders require demonstration of non-Chinese battery content for DOE loan eligibility. Project scale: $5–15B per gigafactory; 10–20 year payback horizon.

Infrastructure Finance (Charging): Infrastructure Finance (Charging): EV charging networks are infrastructure assets financed like utility infrastructure. ChargePoint's SaaS model, EVgo's utility partnerships, and Tesla's Supercharger network (now open to all vehicles) represent different financing models. Charging infrastructure private equity (ArcLight Capital Partners, Brookfield backing charging networks) is an emerging asset class.

Venture Capital: Venture Capital: Autonomous driving (Waymo, Zoox/Amazon, Cruise), next-gen battery technology (QuantumScape, Solid Power), EV software (FreeWire, Moxion Power for mobile charging), and fleet electrification platforms are active VC areas. Robotaxi — if Tesla, Waymo, or a competitor achieves L4 autonomy at scale — is potentially the largest value creation event in transportation since the automobile itself.

Key financial concepts: total cost of ownership (TCO) for fleet electrification, LFP vs. NMC cost/performance tradeoffs, FEOC compliance costs, EV platform amortization (gigafactory depreciation), Robotaxi unit economics (cost per mile vs. human driver), V2G revenue potential, and EV residual values (battery degradation as key driver).

FUTURE OUTLOOK

The EV transition is inevitable — the question is pace, geography, and who wins the manufacturing competition. EVs are expected to exceed 80% of global new car sales by 2040 (EV Volumes). The near-term U.S. headwind from OBBBA credit elimination is real but temporary; rising electricity rates, falling EV prices, and the structural TCO advantage of EVs will sustain long-term growth independent of subsidies. The deeper question is whether Western OEMs can compete with Chinese manufacturers — or whether tariffs and industrial policy simply delay the inevitable reckoning.

Base Case: Base Case: Global EV sales reach ~35–40 million units annually by 2030 (~40% global share). China maintains >50% domestic EV share. U.S. EV market rebounds after OBBBA shock as prices fall and charging infrastructure improves; U.S. share reaches 20–25% by 2030. BYD and Tesla remain #1 and #2 globally. First commercial robotaxi services scale in 3–4 cities.

Bull Case: Bull Case: LFP cost reductions accelerate; solid-state batteries reach commercial production by 2028 (Toyota); Tesla Robotaxi achieves L4 autonomy at scale; V2G creates new revenue streams for EV owners; emerging market EV adoption accelerates via affordable Chinese EVs. Global EV share reaches 60%+ by 2030.

Bear Case: Bear Case: FEOC compliance drives EV prices up; U.S. charging infrastructure builds slowly; consumer range anxiety persists; legacy OEM losses on EVs force production cuts and technology delays. U.S. EV market share stagnates at 15% through 2028; Western OEMs cede emerging markets entirely to Chinese competitors.

The one certainty: the electric drivetrain is structurally superior to the internal combustion engine for most use cases — fewer moving parts, higher efficiency, lower fuel cost, and declining battery prices. The transition will happen. The open questions are timing, the identity of the winners and losers, and whether Western industrial policy can preserve domestic manufacturing of the vehicles that will define 21st-century mobility.
