Project Finance

How a project pays for itself. Who bears the risk.

BriefFull primerInterview prep

5 MIN READ

Project finance is a way to fund an asset through a dedicated project company. Lenders look primarily to that project’s cash flows for repayment, with limited or no recourse to its owners under the agreed structure. [1] The central question is straightforward: can the project produce enough cash, at the right times, to pay its obligations?

The project company connects investors to the operating asset

  • The borrower — imagine a solar project with a total funding requirement of $100 million. Its project company owns the asset and signs the agreements. For this example, lenders provide $70 million and shareholders provide $30 million. These figures are hypothetical, not a recommended financing mix or an estimate for a real solar plant.
  • The participants — a construction contractor builds the plant, an operator maintains it, and a buyer purchases its electricity under our assumed contract. The project company brings those relationships together. This resembles the contractual structure described in the World Bank’s PPP guidance, although a privately contracted solar project is not automatically a public-private partnership. [2]

Construction must finish before electricity sales can repay the loan

  • Funding the build — in our example, construction invoices are paid from committed debt and equity as the work progresses. Electricity sales begin only after the plant is ready. A delay therefore creates a timing problem: money has already been spent, but the operating cash expected to support repayment has not arrived. Interest and other costs may continue during that gap.
  • Covering an overrun — suppose the funding requirement rises from $100 million to $108 million. The extra $8 million needs an identified source. It does not appear simply because a spreadsheet still shows an attractive return. The example could be repaired with additional committed equity, reduced scope where feasible, or another agreed solution. Each choice changes the investment case.

Electricity payments become cash for lenders and shareholders

  • The operating year — assume the completed plant collects $18 million of revenue. Operating costs consume $4 million, and the example allows another $2 million for taxes and other cash adjustments before debt service. That leaves $12 million of cash available for debt service, often abbreviated CFADS. The illustration below shows where the money goes; actual loan definitions and account priorities vary.
  • The payment order — with $8 million due for principal and interest, $4 million remains before any further reserves or distribution restrictions. That residual is not guaranteed dividends. Lenders rank ahead of equity, and shareholders bear the risk that little or no cash remains for them. [4] A project can make its scheduled debt payment while still delivering a disappointing equity outcome.

Coverage measures the cushion above a debt payment

  • One useful ratio — debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR, compares available cash with the payment due for the same period. Here, $12 million divided by $8 million is 1.50 times. It measures headroom rather than the investor’s return. The full primer explains debt sizing and other coverage measures; the brief uses this one calculation to connect operations with financing. [3]
  • A smaller cushion — if collections fall by $2 million with all other assumptions unchanged, available cash falls to $10 million. The same $8 million payment now has only $2 million of headroom. The important lesson is that a change in sales reaches both lenders and shareholders, even when the physical plant still operates. Whether a loan condition is breached depends on the agreement.

A contract can assign a risk without making it disappear

  • Construction risk — a promise to complete the plant helps only if the contractor can perform and the remedy covers the relevant loss. For our hypothetical plant, a delayed completion could reduce sales while a contractor’s compensation is capped. The gap would remain somewhere in the financing plan. This is why risk allocation needs to consider both responsibility and the ability to meet that responsibility. [5]
  • Revenue risk — our electricity buyer might promise a price, but price protection alone does not establish how much electricity the plant will produce or whether the buyer will pay on time. Those are separate questions. A useful investment discussion connects each assumption to a responsible party and asks what cash is available if the assumption fails.

A bankable project is not automatically a good equity investment

The lender needs confidence in repayment; the shareholder needs an attractive outcome for the money invested and the risk taken. [6] For our solar project, the financing story is complete only when construction funding, operating cash, debt payments, and residual distributions fit together. Start a conversation with that sequence, then explain which assumption is most likely to break it.

The project company is the borrower

Project finance separates the financing analysis of an asset from a general loan to its sponsor. The project company is the entity whose cash generation must support the financing. The World Bank describes special-purpose project companies and limited or non-recourse structures. “Limited” matters: support from shareholders may still exist in defined circumstances. [1] A project company is a legal and commercial boundary, not a guarantee that losses cannot reach its owners.

This primer uses one hypothetical solar plant to connect the concepts. Its initial funding requirement is $100 million, met by $70 million of senior debt and $30 million of equity. The figures are teaching assumptions, not a forecast, market leverage benchmark, or investment recommendation. For simplicity, the operating model is stated in one currency and excludes tax credits, subordinated instruments, and complex hedges. The purpose is to show the connections before adding transaction-specific detail.

Start by separating three questions. Who owes the debt? Which cash flows are expected to repay it? What additional support is enforceable if those cash flows are insufficient? For the example, the borrower is Solar Project Company. Its electricity collections are the operating source of repayment. Any completion support would need its own specified provider, amount, conditions, and expiry. It would be misleading to infer unlimited sponsor support from the sponsor’s name or reputation.

A useful comparison is a hypothetical corporate loan to the developer that owns several businesses. That loan would be assessed against the corporate borrower and its obligations; our project loan is assessed around the solar asset and its agreements. Neither label settles whether financing is cheaper, easier, or appropriate. The World Bank identifies corporate finance as an alternative and notes the transaction costs of project structures. [2] The analytical choice is whether separating this asset’s cash flows and risks produces a workable financing package.

The financing mix also describes who carries the residual exposure. Under the example’s priority structure, senior debt has a claim before ordinary equity. Equity can receive the remaining value but cannot assume a scheduled distribution merely because the plant earns revenue. Sponsor support is a separate commitment and may be restricted in time or amount. [4] Keep the equity investment, the project loan, and any additional guarantees distinct when describing the transaction.

Contracts determine who earns payment and who bears loss

The example’s project company connects a construction contractor, an operator, an electricity buyer, lenders, and shareholders. The World Bank’s PPP financing guidance describes construction and operations agreements around a project company. [2] This is a useful structural reference, not a claim that every solar plant has the same contracts. Our solar example is privately contracted and is not designated a PPP.

Assume the construction contract specifies a completion date and performance tests. The revenue model assumes electricity sales after completion. Those two schedules must be compatible: if the plant is delivered late, the forecast must show both the lost operating period and any extra funding need. An analyst should not leave annual revenue unchanged simply because the contractor has promised compensation. Compensation might be disputed, capped, or collected later than the debt payment it was meant to protect.

For the electricity purchase agreement, distinguish price, quantity, and payment timing. Suppose the price is fixed but sales depend on actual production. The agreement then does not turn low production into full revenue. If payment is delayed, recognized revenue does not solve a cash shortfall today. In this hypothetical, an invoice paid three months late changes liquidity even when the final annual amount is unchanged. The model must represent when money reaches the bank account.

The same discipline applies to the operator. A performance obligation should be connected to a measurable service requirement and a remedy. In our example, replacing a failed component might restore production, while a financial claim against the operator might arrive later. These are different recovery paths. The risk-allocation principle is to consider which party can manage the risk effectively, together with the practical consequences when performance fails. [5]

A useful contract review produces a short list of gaps rather than a long inventory of document names. For each gap, state the cash consequence, the proposed remedy, and the exposure that remains. A risk has not disappeared because a contract assigns it to someone. For the solar plant, the decisive question is whether that assignment restores cash or performance before the financing runs out of headroom. This is an analytical framework; actual rights require review of the signed agreements and applicable law.

Construction funding must cover both cost and timing

The hypothetical $100 million funding requirement should be treated as a complete sources-and-uses exercise. In the example, assume $90 million of construction and equipment costs, $5 million of development and financing costs, and $5 million of contingency. These invented allocations sum to the funding requirement; they do not describe a typical solar cost breakdown. The $70 million loan and $30 million equity commitment are the sources. Counting a contingency as both an expense and an additional source would overstate available funding.

Timing matters as much as the final total. Imagine $20 million is due to suppliers in a particular month while only $15 million can be drawn from committed sources under the model’s assumptions. The project has a $5 million liquidity gap even if total lifetime sources equal total uses. A financing model should therefore show periodic payments and draws, not just a summary at completion. An unfulfilled condition to borrowing cannot be treated as cash in the account.

Now increase the total requirement to $108 million, holding the original sources fixed. The deficit is $8 million. If additional equity is the chosen solution, total equity becomes $38 million and the shareholder’s future distributions must be assessed against that larger investment. If additional debt is proposed instead, the operating cash must support the increased repayment burden. Neither solution can be assumed without revising the commitments and the financial analysis.

A schedule delay creates a second pathway to a deficit. Suppose the example’s plant begins operating one quarter later. Revenue that was expected during that quarter moves or disappears, depending on the buyer agreement and the remaining term. Costs incurred during the delay must be funded. Do not automatically recover all missed production by extending the forecast: that would require a justified extension of the project’s economic or contractual life.

For a recruiting discussion, explain the sequence: the delay changes the cash schedule; the changed schedule creates a funding need; a specified party must cover that need. Then identify the document or operating evidence that would validate the solution. This is more informative than saying construction is risky. The risk-allocation source discusses completion cost, timing, and performance as distinct concerns. [5] Our example translates those categories into a cash requirement without assigning a real-world probability to them.

Operating cash pays obligations before equity distributions

Use cash collections, not a headline profit measure, to start the example’s operating-year bridge. Assume $18 million is collected from electricity sales, $4 million pays operating costs, and $2 million covers taxes and other adjustments included before debt service in this simplified model. Cash available for debt service is therefore $12 million. The category definitions are assumptions of this exercise; they must be replaced with the financing agreement’s definitions in a real model.

Scheduled principal and interest are $8 million. Subtracting that payment leaves $4 million before any additional reserves, cash retention, or distribution restrictions. The figure is deliberately called a residual, not dividends. A cash waterfall specifies the order in which funds are applied. The World Bank distinguishes senior debt, subordinated capital, and equity priority. [6] Our simplified waterfall does not claim to reproduce the exact account order in any loan agreement.

Debt service coverage ratio is a period measure of available cash relative to interest and principal payments. [3] For the example, $12m ÷ $8m = 1.50×. This is a measure of headroom. It is not a 50% shareholder return, and the $4 million difference is not proof that distributions are permitted. Keep the numerator, denominator, period, and account rules visible whenever quoting the ratio.

A revenue sensitivity makes the mechanism concrete. If annual collections decline by $2 million and all modeled costs stay unchanged, available cash becomes $10 million, coverage becomes 1.25×, and the residual before further restrictions becomes $2 million. The operating asset may still function while the financial cushion halves. If collections instead fall by $5 million, available cash is $7 million against $8 million due: the example then has a $1 million payment shortfall before using reserves or new support.

Timing can be worse than the annual average suggests. Suppose the base case still collects $18 million over the year, but a large invoice arrives after a scheduled payment date. The annual calculation alone will miss the interim shortage. For the example, test the payment periods used by the loan and keep an opening cash balance, receipts, payments, and closing balance for each. That exercise separates economic weakness from a temporary mismatch, while still recognizing that either can require a funded solution.

Debt size depends on the repayment schedule

The $70 million loan in our opening example is an assumed source, not an amount established by the one-year coverage calculation. To justify a loan size, build a repayment schedule with an opening balance, interest, principal repayment, and closing balance for each period. A model that shows $8 million of debt service forever without checking the remaining balance has not demonstrated when the loan is repaid.

Suppose an illustrative lender requires 1.40× coverage in a particular year. With $12 million of modeled available cash, debt service for that year could be no greater than about $8.57 million under that constraint: $12m ÷ 1.40. This is a hypothetical sizing assumption, not a market threshold. It sets a period payment limit, not the loan’s initial principal. Interest rate, maturity, amortization, other constraints, and cash in the other years still determine the feasible debt amount.

Debt sculpting can be understood as fitting payments to the modeled cash pattern while satisfying the chosen coverage constraints. In an illustrative year with only $9.8 million of available cash, the same 1.40× assumption supports $7 million of debt service. A later, stronger year may support more. Moving principal to later years may help earlier coverage, but it also changes outstanding balances and financing costs. The entire schedule must still repay the loan by the assumed maturity or explicitly model the remaining balance.

Loan life coverage ratio considers discounted available cash through loan maturity relative to debt outstanding. [3] If the hypothetical present value of that cash is $91 million and the loan outstanding is $70 million, LLCR is 1.30×. This is a separate invented valuation assumption; it is not derived from the single operating year above. A multi-year coverage measure and a period payment measure answer different questions. A comfortable total can coexist with a difficult payment date.

Refinancing is another assumption to expose. If the example leaves principal outstanding at maturity, a replacement lender or another funding source must repay it. A model should show the amount, date, and financing terms needed. Stress those assumptions rather than treating the refinancing as automatic. For a clear interview answer, separate the operating case from the financing case: the plant’s cash generation may be unchanged while a shorter maturity or higher interest cost makes the proposed loan unworkable.

Lender protections need practical recovery paths

Financing agreements can constrain distributions, require cash retention, and give lenders intervention rights when agreed conditions are not met. Direct agreements may establish notice, cure, or substitution arrangements with key counterparties. [3] The existence of a right does not establish that exercising it will restore the example’s production or cash in time. A useful analysis connects the contractual right to an operational recovery plan.

Suppose our model has an assumed distribution test of 1.30×. The downside case at 1.25× could then retain cash even though the $8 million payment is still made. This invented test illustrates why a distribution restriction and a payment default are different events. Do not label 1.30× a universal covenant or infer an automatic remedy: the document would specify the calculation, testing date, consequence, and any cure mechanism.

Consider an operating failure instead. A replacement operator might restore output, but changing operators requires a workable transition: technical information, site access, staff, licenses where relevant, and cash for repairs. These are diligence questions for our hypothetical project, not a prediction of a particular legal process. A financing model that assumes instant recovery would hide both the delay and the cost. Model a realistic period without normal receipts and identify who funds the restart.

Multiple capital providers create another coordination question. If the example added a subordinated loan, the parties would need to understand payment priority and how their rights interact. The World Bank describes intercreditor arrangements and equity’s lower priority. [4] For the hypothetical analysis, test whether one party’s proposed enforcement action would preserve or destroy the operating cash flow on which everyone depends. Coordination cannot be inferred from simply labeling debt senior and junior.

Before treating support as protection, ask whether it is committed, available when needed, and large enough for the scenario. An $8 million committed overrun facility might address the construction gap calculated earlier but say nothing about a later buyer default. An insurance policy or guarantee would similarly need its own covered event, exclusions, limit, and claims timing checked. The important model discipline is to avoid using the same assumed source twice to cure independent cash needs.

Equity returns and lending decisions use different tests

The shareholder’s $30 million contribution is an outflow at specified dates. Its return depends on the timing and amount of later distributions and any net sale proceeds. The annual $4 million residual in our base case is not an IRR. To calculate an equity IRR, construct the full series of equity contributions and distributions after financing, with a clearly stated treatment of any terminal value. A price change can alter equity return even if the plant’s operating performance is identical.

For example, imagine an investor pays an extra $5 million to acquire the same equity cash flows. With no other changes, that extra outflow worsens the investment result. Alternatively, the $8 million construction overrun might require equity to rise from $30 million to $38 million while future cash stays unchanged. These are two different reasons for a lower return: a higher acquisition price and additional project funding. Keeping them separate makes the analysis easier to challenge and explain.

Leverage also needs both sides of the calculation. Replacing some equity with more debt reduces the initial equity check but adds future financing obligations. The result depends on the added debt’s price, timing, and risk. In the example, an extra fixed payment makes the same decline in revenue more painful for the residual equity cash flow. A model should not assume that increasing debt always improves an acceptable investment outcome.

The recruiting application depends on the role. A lending discussion can begin with the cash available for each payment and the mechanism for recovering from a shortfall. An equity discussion can begin with purchase price, contributions, distributions, and the downside to those distributions. An advisory discussion can explain the gap between the project’s funding need and the capital its contracts and cash flows can support. These are editorial ways to organize an answer, not claims about a specific employer’s process.

A strong closing answer for the solar example is conditional: the financing works if construction can be fully funded, the plant meets its operating assumptions, collections arrive when needed, and the debt schedule fits the resulting cash. The equity investment needs an additional test: are the expected distributions attractive relative to the money committed and the downside? That distinction helps avoid confusing a useful infrastructure asset, a financeable project, and a good purchase.

Before using this primer for a live transaction, replace every illustrative number and contractual assumption with project evidence. This edition intentionally omits current lending spreads, tax incentives, and jurisdiction-specific enforcement rules. Those omissions keep the introductory framework usable without implying that generic guidance establishes the economics or legal outcome of a real deal. The references support the core concepts; the worked project and recruiting exercises are original teaching analysis.

Interview prep

Start with industry basics, then explore business economics and investment judgment.

Industry basics

What makes a financing project finance?

Repayment depends primarily on a dedicated project’s cash flows.

The borrower and recourse structure matter more than the industry label. In our solar example, the project company borrows and electricity collections support repayment. The World Bank describes limited or non-recourse structures. [1] I would separately identify any sponsor support rather than assuming the parent has no obligations or guarantees everything.

Follow-up: Which document tells you when sponsor support expires?

How is project finance different from corporate finance?

The financing is organized around the project company rather than the sponsor’s general business.

For our example, I would compare lending to Solar Project Company with lending to its developer. The first analysis follows the plant’s contracts and cash. The second begins with the corporate borrower. The World Bank presents both financing routes. [2] The label alone does not determine which structure is cheaper or better.

Follow-up: When might the cost of a separate project structure outweigh its benefits?

Who provides capital and who receives payment?

Debt and equity fund construction; operating cash then meets obligations before residual equity distributions.

Our hypothetical sources are $70 million of debt and $30 million of equity. The diagram separates that initial funding from the annual cash waterfall. The electricity buyer is a revenue counterparty, not an equity investor in this example. Keeping those roles separate prevents confusion between a financing inflow and earned operating cash.

Follow-up: Why is revenue not the same as cash available for shareholders?

Does a fixed electricity price eliminate revenue risk?

No. Production and collection can still differ from the forecast.

In our example, assume the buyer pays a fixed price for electricity actually delivered. Lower output would still reduce sales, and delayed buyer payment would still reduce cash available today. I would explain which risk the assumed contract addresses and which assumptions remain exposed rather than calling the entire revenue stream guaranteed.

Follow-up: What happens if the plant produces normally but the buyer pays late?

Business economics

Calculate the project’s DSCR.

The base case is $12 million divided by $8 million, or 1.50 times.

The $12 million is after the operating costs and other pre-debt adjustments defined in our exercise; the $8 million includes principal and interest. DSCR is a period coverage measure. [3] I would use the same period for both quantities and would not describe the result as an equity return.

Follow-up: What is coverage if available cash falls to $10 million?

Can you derive the initial loan size from one year’s DSCR?

No. You also need the complete repayment schedule and its constraints.

The example assumes a $70 million loan; it does not establish that size from the $12 million operating cash figure. At a hypothetical 1.40 times requirement, that year supports about $8.57 million of debt service. Interest, maturity, principal repayments, and cash in other years still determine the opening debt that can be repaid.

Follow-up: What changes if the loan must repay two years earlier?

How does LLCR differ from DSCR?

LLCR considers discounted cash through loan maturity; DSCR focuses on a payment period.

The World Bank distinguishes these two measures. [3] In our separate illustrative valuation assumption, $91 million of discounted available cash divided by $70 million outstanding gives 1.30 times LLCR. A comfortable lifetime total can still coexist with a shortfall at a particular payment date, so I would not substitute one measure for the other.

Follow-up: Could annual DSCR hide a shortage at a semiannual payment date?

What happens if construction costs rise by $8 million?

The model needs $8 million of additional committed funding or another credible adjustment.

Original sources of $100 million no longer meet uses of $108 million. If equity covers the gap, the contribution rises from $30 million to $38 million. If debt is proposed, repayment capacity must be retested. I would identify who is obligated to fund the deficit and when that money can be drawn.

Follow-up: How would a delay change the cash need beyond the extra construction invoice?

Investment judgment

Why can a bankable project be a poor equity investment?

Debt repayment capacity does not establish an attractive return at the price paid.

The solar plant might cover scheduled payments while leaving modest or delayed distributions. Equity investors must compare those cash flows with their contributions and acquisition price. Paying more for the same future distributions worsens the investment result. My answer would distinguish the lender’s payment test from the shareholder’s return analysis.

Follow-up: Which assumptions would you challenge before increasing the equity purchase price?

Does assigning a risk to a contractor remove it?

Only to the extent the contractor can perform and the remedy covers the loss.

For the hypothetical plant, delay compensation might be capped or arrive after a debt payment. The project could therefore retain a financing gap despite a contractual claim. The risk-allocation guidance emphasizes management of construction and operating risks. [5] I would connect the remedy to the cash and operational consequences, not stop at the contract label.

Follow-up: What remains exposed if the responsible contractor becomes insolvent?

Is a distribution lockup the same as payment default?

No. A project may pay its lenders while retaining cash that shareholders expected to receive.

In our illustrative downside case, coverage is 1.25 times. An assumed 1.30 times distribution test could block dividends even though the scheduled debt payment is met. That threshold is invented for teaching. The actual definition, consequence, and any cure must come from the loan agreement.

Follow-up: How would you distinguish a cash-flow problem from a temporary timing mismatch?

What would make you reject the financing case?

An unresolved funding gap or a repayment assumption that the evidence cannot support.

For our solar example, I would trace the weakest assumption through the cash schedule. If an overrun has no committed source, or repayment depends on an unexplained refinancing, the financing story is incomplete. I would state what evidence or structural change could resolve the issue rather than rejecting the project because of a generic risk label.

Follow-up: What single change would most improve the downside without hiding its cost?

Coffee chat questions
  • Which assumption most often changes between the first financing model and financial close?
  • How does your team decide whether a risk needs a contract remedy, more equity, or a smaller loan?
  • What operating evidence would make you reconsider an otherwise strong financing case?
